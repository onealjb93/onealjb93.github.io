Loess Plateau, China

Farmers terraced a moonscape back to green — cutting the sediment flowing to the Yellow River by an estimated 110 million tonnes a year.
LANDSCAPE RESTORATION

China’s Loess Plateau was one of the most eroded places on Earth, its bare hills shedding soil into the Yellow River — until farmers terraced the slopes and planted them green again.

Slopes terraced and planted → soil stays on the hills → harvests rise and the river runs clearer

Proof: in the project areas, grain harvests rose by more than half. GOVERNMENT-REPORTED

ENVIRONMENT

Restored vegetation and reduced erosion.

ECONOMY

Terraced slopes that feed the families who farm them.

EQUITY

2.5 million people rose out of poverty.

Details

Between 1994 and 2005, World Bank-backed projects helped farmers terrace the slopes, plant the hills with trees and grass, and dam the gullies — across some 35,000 square kilometres. The sediment flowing toward the Yellow River fell by an estimated 110 million tonnes a year, and 2.5 million people rose out of poverty.

One family’s 5,000 acres near Bismarck, kept in family farming for the long term.

Costa Rica

Pay landowners to keep trees standing, and the forest returns — from 21% of the country to more than half.
FOREST AND ECOSYSTEM RESTORATION

Costa Rica cleared its forest for cattle until only about a fifth of the country was still wooded — then it began paying landowners to keep trees standing and let them grow back.

Landowners paid to protect and replant → trees left standing → forest cover climbing again

Proof: forest cover has climbed from about 21% in 1987 to more than half the country — one of the few deforestation reversals in the world. GOVERNMENT-REPORTED

ENVIRONMENT

The forest came back — from a fifth of the country to more than half, one of the few deforestation reversals in the world.

ECONOMY

Landowners are paid for the work their standing forest does for everyone — funded largely by a small tax on fossil fuels.

EQUITY

The payments go to the landowners who keep the trees standing — the people doing the protecting are the people paid.

The paying began in 1997 — the PSA programme — payments for ecosystem services, the work a standing forest does for everyone: holding water, cleaning air, sheltering wildlife — run by the national forestry fund FONAFIFO and paid for largely by a small tax on fossil fuels.

Industrial agriculture

Five strands pull soil organic matter out of the line, and no strand returns soil organic matter to the line. The line drops in a step at each of the five strands, and each step leaves the line lower than the level before the step. The soil band under the line ends half as thick as the soil band begins, and the amount of soil organic matter falls year after year.

Regenerative agriculture

Three full circles carry soil organic matter around and feed soil organic matter back into the line. After each circle, the line climbs to a higher level than the level before the circle. The start is the hardest part: the line climbs least before the first circle closes. The soil band under the line ends twice as thick as the soil band begins, and the amount of soil organic matter rises year after year.

Soil organic matter is the carbon-rich remains of living things in the soil. Regenerative agriculture is the switch: from industrial farming, which depletes soil and ecological health, to farming that builds both. The checkable label is Regenerative Organic Certified, run by the Regenerative Organic Alliance — a farm must first hold United States Department of Agriculture (USDA) organic certification, then pass added standards on soil health, animal welfare, and fairness to farmers and farm workers. The long proof is the Rodale Institute’s Farming Systems Trial in Pennsylvania, which has compared organic and conventional grain farming side by side since 1981. In the trial, the organic systems’ soil health has kept rising while the conventional system’s stayed essentially unchanged — and in drought years, the organic corn out-yielded the conventional corn by 31 percent.

Westland, the Netherlands

A glass city growing up to 80 kilos of tomatoes on one square metre — on as little as 4 litres of water per kilo.
THE WATER LOOP

Westland, between The Hague and Rotterdam, is a glass city: greenhouse after greenhouse growing tomatoes, peppers and cucumbers for all of Europe — almost nothing of it in soil.

Rain caught from the roof → fed drop by drop to roots in slabs → drained, cleaned, and fed again

Proof: the Netherlands — a country smaller than West Virginia — is the world’s second-largest exporter of farm goods by value, behind only the United States (€128.9 billion in 2024, CBS). GOVERNMENT-REPORTED

Most Dutch glasshouses still burn natural gas for heat, using its CO₂ for the plants — softening the cost, not erasing it; cutting that dependence is the industry’s unfinished work.

ENVIRONMENT

Rain caught from the roofs and fed drop by drop; what drains away is disinfected and fed again.

ECONOMY

The Netherlands — smaller than West Virginia — is the world’s second-largest exporter of farm goods by value.

EQUITY

Food grown in abundance where land is scarcest — between The Hague and Rotterdam, feeding all of Europe.

Details

Roots sit in slabs of stone wool and are fed drop by drop with water carrying exactly the food the plant needs; what drains away is disinfected and fed again, and rain is caught from the roofs and stored. Computers steer vents, screens and heating hour by hour, and waste CO₂ from Rotterdam’s refineries is piped in — the OCAP loop, told in the Material chapter — to make the plants grow faster. Wageningen researchers have measured the result: up to 80 kilos of tomatoes from a square metre in a year, on as little as 4 litres of water per kilo, where an open field can take 60 litres or more.

Sundrop Farms, Port Augusta

Tomatoes in the desert, grown on concentrated sunlight and seawater — no river, no borehole, almost no rain.
SUNLIGHT AND SEAWATER

At Port Augusta, in country too dry to farm, Sundrop grows tomatoes in twenty hectares of glasshouses — made from sunlight and seawater, a short walk from the sea.

Sunlight focused by mirrors → heat makes fresh water from the sea and warms the glass → tomatoes grow where nothing could

Proof: the engineering firm that built the energy system, Aalborg CSP, counts 250,000 cubic metres of seawater desalinated a year — fresh water manufactured on site, in a place with almost none. COMPANY-REPORTED

The farm has changed hands twice since it opened — to Morrison & Co in 2019, then to Centuria funds in 2022 — and it is still growing tomatoes today.

ENVIRONMENT

A desert that grows food — 15,000 tonnes of truss tomatoes a year, from sunlight and the sea.

ECONOMY

250,000 cubic metres of seawater desalinated a year — fresh water manufactured on site, in a place with almost none.

EQUITY

Fresh food produced at Port Augusta, in country with almost no fresh water to spare.

Details

Port Augusta, at the head of South Australia’s Spencer Gulf, has hot sun, almost no rain, and no fresh water to spare. More than 23,000 mirrors track the sun and focus it onto one tower; the heat desalinates seawater for the crop, warms the glasshouses through cold desert nights, and generates a little electricity besides. Inside, the tomatoes grow hydroponically — roots in nutrient water, never soil — sheltered from the desert outside. The farm opened in 2016 and produces more than 15,000 tonnes of truss tomatoes a year, sold through Coles supermarkets across Australia.

The Weather, Moved Indoors

Where it belongs: indoors

Where farming outside cannot work — weather too harsh, or land too scarce where people live densely — the farm moves under cover.

The grower sets the weather

Under cover — in a glasshouse or an indoor farm — the grower, not the weather, decides the light, the warmth, the water and the feed.

The water runs in a loop

Roots sit in water or in clean slabs instead of soil, and what drains away is caught, cleaned and used again — a fraction of a field’s water, many times its yield from the same ground, and a harvest in every month of the year.

That loop is hydroponics: water carrying the nutrients circulates past the roots, the runoff is collected, cleaned, and reused — nothing drains away to the field.

This is the indoor half of the same push regenerative agriculture makes outdoors — and because the grower controls the whole environment, the standard is high: highly productive, with good jobs for people. The science is led by Wageningen University & Research in the Netherlands, and the heartland is the Dutch glasshouse belt. The price is electricity: a fully indoor farm buys every ray of its light, and a Wageningen study found an indoor farm needed about three and a half times the electricity of a Dutch glasshouse to grow the same lettuce. When power prices rose, that bill broke Plenty, Bowery and AppHarvest in 2023–25. The examples below are the ones still standing.

Flexionics, Boden, Sweden

A warehouse of miners in Swedish Lapland that Sweden’s grid called on more than 11,000 times in a year — and pays to switch off.
FLEXIBLE DEMAND

In Boden, Swedish Lapland, a warehouse of Bitcoin miners works for Sweden’s grid first and mines second — powering up or down the moment the grid calls.

Too much power → the miners soak it up → too little → the miners switch off in about a second

Proof: the company counts 11,245 calls in one year, and says grid services now earn more than mining. COMPANY-REPORTED

Every figure in this example is the company’s own.

ENVIRONMENT

Miners soak up surplus wind and hydro power, then switch off in about a second when the grid runs short.

ECONOMY

Grid services now earn the company more than mining — 11,245 calls answered in one year.

EQUITY

A warehouse in Swedish Lapland working for Sweden’s grid first — balancing power for every home on it.

Details

Flexionics Energy, a Swiss company, runs about 14 megawatts of miners at Boden and Utansjö — a draw big enough to matter to a grid, in machines that can change it at once. The sites sell balancing services to Svenska kraftnät, Sweden’s national grid operator: when wind and hydro power overshoot or fall short, the operator pays users who can shift their demand on the spot — a service called demand response — and Flexionics says its machines answer in under a second. In the year covered by Joe Nakamoto’s film Inside Sweden’s Strangest Bitcoin Mine — a film Flexionics sponsored, and says so — 58% of income came from grid services rather than bitcoin. Even worn-out “Frankenstein” miners earn their keep here: flexibility does not need the newest machines.

Duke University’s Nicholas Institute calculates, in its February 2025 report Rethinking Load Growth, that the United States grid could add 76 gigawatts of new load — about a tenth of its peak demand — with no new power stations or grid upgrades. The condition: the new load must be able to switch off for up to 0.25% of the year, about 22 hours. The report is about flexible load in general, not Bitcoin. A mine is the rare load that meets its condition — it switches off in seconds, and nothing spoils.

Six bitcoin servers stand among a Dutch grower’s tulip beds — their heat grows the tulips, dries the bulbs, and cuts the gas bill.

Details

How the grip works. Each toe hair is called a seta (say “SEE-tee”), and every seta ends in hundreds of finer tips. Pressed against a surface, the tips come so close that the faint pull between molecules — van der Waals forces — adds up over millions of contacts into one powerful grip. Nothing is sticky, and nothing is left behind.

One-way, and self-cleaning. The grip points one way. Drag a toe along a surface and the hairs lock on; roll the toe upward and they peel free — that rolling peel is how a gecko takes each step. The hairs are keratin, the stuff of fingernails, packed about five thousand to a square millimetre of toe, and the feet clean themselves as they walk: dirt clings to the wall more strongly than to the hairs, so a few steps shed it.

Why space wants it. The pull needs no air, so it holds in a vacuum — where ordinary glue would freeze, dry out, or poison the cabin air.

The latest test, and the copy on sale. In 2024 an Astrobee robot on the space station was put through its paces with gecko-pad gripping arms (the REACCH test), practising catching satellites. Products Designed as Nutrients

Waste = food. In nature nothing is thrown away — what one living thing leaves behind feeds another. Cradle to Cradle designs products the same way: every material is food for one of two cycles, and which cycle is decided before the product is made.

Biological nutrients — food for the soil

Products of consumption — things that get used up, like food packaging and natural fibres. Their materials are safe to rot: after use they decompose, become soil, and feed new growth.

Technical nutrients — food for industry

Products of service — things used but not used up, like a chair, a carpet, a machine part. Their metals and high-quality materials are returned, taken apart and remade at the same quality — never downcycled into lesser goods, and never leaked into the soil.

The model is the book’s cherry tree: it makes thousands of blossoms, far more than it needs, and none of it is waste — what falls decomposes and feeds the soil, the insects and the birds. Not efficient. Effective.

The book states the method as three principles: waste = food; use current solar income — run on today’s sunlight, not stored carbon; and celebrate diversity — design that fits its place.

The idea comes from William McDonough and Michael Braungart’s 2002 book Cradle to Cradle: Remaking the Way We Make Things. The certificate is a separate thing, run by the non-profit Cradle to Cradle Products Innovation Institute, which the two authors founded: the Institute scores products and materials against the book’s principles under the current standard, Version 5.0 — and it does not certify buildings. A certificate runs three years before the product is assessed again, and certified products can contribute toward LEED credits.

What the certificate certifies is this: every material in the product is safe enough, and designed well enough, to be food for one of two cycles. Biological nutrients return to the soil. Technical nutrients circle in industry at full quality. The Circular Economy names the whole system; Cradle to Cradle certifies the products inside it, one product at a time.

Regenerative Building Standard

What it is

The Living Building Challenge asks a building to work like a flower: gather its own sunlight and water, waste nothing, and give back more than it takes. It is run by the International Living Future Institute, a non-profit, and has been running since 2006.

The Institute’s model is a flower with seven Petals — the seven areas a building must get right: Place, Water, Energy, Health + Happiness, Materials, Equity and Beauty. Between them the Petals hold 20 Imperatives — the individual must-do requirements.

Why it works

No building passes on drawings. Before any certificate, the Institute audits 12 straight months of the building’s real, measured performance — a full year of proof that it actually lives this way.

What it asks of a building

The full standard — called Living — means all 20 Imperatives. Two shorter paths exist: Core means the 10 Imperatives marked ★, the ten every path requires; Petal certification means those 10 Core plus every Imperative in one whole Petal — Water, Energy or Materials.

Details — the 20 Imperatives

What a Living Building must actually do, grouped by Petal. ★ marks a Core Imperative — one of the ten every certification path requires.

Place · 4 Imperatives — restoring a healthy interrelationship with nature.
  • 01 Ecology of Place ★ — Protect and restore the land the building sits on; never build on wild or prime farm land.
  • 02 Urban Agriculture — Grow food on part of the site.
  • 03 Habitat Exchange — Protect an equal area of wild land somewhere else, permanently.
  • 04 Human-Scaled Living ★ — Build compact and walkable, sized for people, not cars.
Water · 2 Imperatives — developments that operate within the water balance of a given place and climate.
  • 05 Responsible Water Use ★ — Use far less drinking water than a typical building.
  • 06 Net Positive Water — Catch, treat and reuse all the water the building needs, on its own site.
Energy · 2 Imperatives — relying only on current solar income.
  • 07 Energy + Carbon Reduction ★ — Use far less energy than a typical building, and cut carbon with it.
  • 08 Net Positive Carbon — Make more renewable energy on site than the building uses, and offset the carbon spent building it.
Health + Happiness · 3 Imperatives — environments that optimize physical and psychological health and well-being.
  • 09 Healthy Interior Environment ★ — Fresh air, daylight and a view out for everyone inside.
  • 10 Healthy Interior Performance — Test the indoor air after move-in, and keep it clean.
  • 11 Access to Nature — Put nature within everyone’s reach, inside and out.

  • 14 Responsible Sourcing — Buy wood and materials from certified, responsible sources.
  • 15 Living Economy Sourcing — Buy from makers and suppliers near the site.
  • 16 Net Positive Waste — Send almost nothing to landfill, in building or in use.

  • 17 Universal Access ★ — Design so everyone can enter, use and enjoy the building.
