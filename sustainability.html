The line drops in a step at each of the five strands, and each step leaves the line lower than the level before the step. The soil band under the line ends half as thick as the soil band begins, and the amount of soil organic matter falls year after year.

Regenerative agriculture

Three full circles carry soil organic matter around and feed soil organic matter back into the line. After each circle, the line climbs to a higher level than the level before the circle. The start is the hardest part: the line climbs least before the first circle closes. The soil band under the line ends twice as thick as the soil band begins, and the amount of soil organic matter rises year after year.

Soil organic matter is the carbon-rich remains of living things in the soil. Regenerative agriculture is the switch: from industrial farming, which depletes soil and ecological health, to farming that builds both. The checkable label is Regenerative Organic Certified, run by the Regenerative Organic Alliance — a farm must first hold United States Department of Agriculture (USDA) organic certification, then pass added standards on soil health, animal welfare, and fairness to farmers and farm workers. The long proof is the Rodale Institute’s Farming Systems Trial in Pennsylvania, which has compared organic and conventional grain farming side by side since 1981: soil health in the trial’s organic systems has kept rising while the conventional system’s stayed essentially unchanged, and organic corn yields ran 31 percent higher than conventional corn yields in drought years.

China’s Loess Plateau was one of the most eroded places on Earth, its bare hills shedding soil into the Yellow River — until farmers terraced the slopes and planted them green again.

Slopes terraced and planted → soil stays on the hills → harvests rise and the river runs clearer

Proof: in the project areas, grain harvests rose by more than half.

Details

Between 1994 and 2005, World Bank-backed projects helped farmers terrace the slopes, plant the hills with trees and grass, and dam the gullies — across some 35,000 square kilometres. The sediment flowing toward the Yellow River fell by an estimated 110 million tonnes a year, and 2.5 million people rose out of poverty.

Gabe Brown farms some 5,000 acres near Bismarck, North Dakota, where hail wiping out his crops twice pushed him to stop fighting his soil and start feeding it.

Worn soil → cover crops, no-till, cattle → organic matter from under 2% to roughly 5–8% → rain soaks in instead of running off

Proof: rain that once soaked in at half an inch an hour now soaks in at up to eight inches an hour.

Details

The method: no ploughing, a changing mix of cover crops on every field, and cattle moved through in tight herds, the way bison once crossed the prairie. The untouched prairie nearby still holds 7–8% organic matter — the soil that was there before farming, and the mark his fields are closing on.

ButternutHoneynut

In 2009, chef Dan Barber put a challenge to Cornell plant breeder Michael Mazourek: breed a butternut squash that actually tastes good.

Chef asks for flavour → breeder selects for taste, not size → a smaller, sweeter squash people seek out

Proof: the squash started a company — in 2018 Barber and Mazourek co-founded Row 7 Seed Company, breeding vegetables for flavour first.

Details

The supermarket butternut is bred big, uniform and hard to bruise — and comes out watery and bland. Working from a butternut–buttercup cross first made at Cornell in the 1980s, Mazourek selected plant by plant for flavour, with Barber tasting as he went. The result broke the supermarket brief: half the size, thin edible skin, flesh so concentrated it needs no sweetening — and two to three times a butternut’s beta-carotene.

A farmer works KickStart’s MoneyMaker pump like a small stair-stepper, drawing water from as deep as seven metres onto fields that would otherwise sit dry until the rains.

Pump sold, not donated → farmer irrigates in the dry season → a second growing season every year

Proof: more than 370,000 pumps are now in use, and the Skoll Foundation counts 1.4 million people lifted a major step out of poverty.

Details

The water comes from a stream, a well, or a pond, and the farmer spreads it over the fields. The Super MoneyMaker sells for about $100 — real money in rural Kenya. It is sold, never donated, on purpose: a pump a farmer has paid for gets used, repaired and resold. What it buys is a second harvest — crops grown in the dry season, when prices are highest.

Costa Rica cleared its forest for cattle until only about a fifth of the country was still wooded — then it began paying landowners to keep trees standing and let them grow back.

Landowners paid to protect and replant → trees left standing → forest cover climbing again

Proof: forest cover has climbed from about 21% in 1987 to more than half the country — one of the few deforestation reversals in the world.

Details

The fire is not a metaphor: the photograph shows a real fire in Puntarenas in 2009, and the burning was the country's real history — forest burned and cleared for cattle pasture until, by the 1980s, only about a fifth was still forest. The change since is just as real. The paying began in 1997 — the PSA programme (payments for ecosystem services), run by the national forestry fund FONAFIFO and paid for largely by a small tax on fossil fuels.

The Weather, Moved Indoors

Where it belongs: indoors

Where farming outside cannot work — weather too harsh, or land too scarce where people live densely — the farm moves under cover.

The grower sets the weather

Under cover — in a glasshouse or an indoor farm — the grower, not the weather, decides the light, the warmth, the water and the feed.

The water runs in a loop

Roots sit in water or in clean slabs instead of soil, and what drains away is caught, cleaned and used again — a fraction of a field’s water, many times its yield from the same ground, and a harvest in every month of the year.

This is the indoor half of the same push regenerative agriculture makes outdoors — and because the grower controls the whole environment, the standard is high: highly productive, with good jobs for people. The science is led by Wageningen University & Research in the Netherlands, and the heartland is the Dutch glasshouse belt. The price is electricity: a fully indoor farm buys every ray of its light, and a Wageningen study found an indoor farm needed about three and a half times the electricity of a Dutch glasshouse to grow the same lettuce. When power prices rose, that bill broke Plenty, Bowery and AppHarvest in 2023–25. The examples below are the ones still standing.

Westland, between The Hague and Rotterdam, is a glass city: greenhouse after greenhouse growing tomatoes, peppers and cucumbers for all of Europe — almost nothing of it in soil.

Rain caught from the roof → fed drop by drop to roots in slabs → drained, cleaned, and fed again

Proof: the Netherlands — a country smaller than West Virginia — is the world’s second-largest exporter of farm goods by value, behind only the United States (€128.9 billion in 2024, CBS).

Most Dutch glasshouses still burn natural gas for heat, using its CO₂ for the plants — softening the cost, not erasing it; cutting that dependence is the industry’s unfinished work.

Details

Roots sit in slabs of stone wool and are fed drop by drop with water carrying exactly the food the plant needs; what drains away is disinfected and fed again, and rain is caught from the roofs and stored. Computers steer vents, screens and heating hour by hour, and waste CO₂ from Rotterdam’s refineries is piped in — the OCAP loop told earlier on this page — to make the plants grow faster. Wageningen researchers have measured the result: up to 80 kilos of tomatoes from a square metre in a year, on as little as 4 litres of water per kilo, where an open field can take 60 litres or more.

Learn more: Wageningen University & Research ↗

At Port Augusta, in country too dry to farm, Sundrop grows tomatoes in twenty hectares of glasshouses — made from sunlight and seawater, a short walk from the sea.

Sunlight focused by mirrors → heat makes fresh water from the sea and warms the glass → tomatoes grow where nothing could

Proof: the engineering firm that built the energy system, Aalborg CSP, counts 250,000 cubic metres of seawater desalinated a year — fresh water manufactured on site, in a place with almost none.

The farm has changed hands twice since it opened — to Morrison & Co in 2019, then to Centuria funds in 2022 — and it is still growing tomatoes today.

Details

Port Augusta, at the head of South Australia’s Spencer Gulf, has hot sun, almost no rain, and no fresh water to spare. More than 23,000 mirrors track the sun and focus it onto one tower; the heat desalinates seawater for the crop, warms the glasshouses through cold desert nights, and generates a little electricity besides. Inside, the tomatoes grow hydroponically — roots in nutrient water, never soil — sheltered from the desert outside. The farm opened in 2016 and produces more than 15,000 tonnes of truss tomatoes a year, sold through Coles supermarkets across Australia.

Spread grows lettuce in sealed Kyoto rooms, stacked in layers under LED light — no soil, no weather, no pesticides — and, rare in this field, it makes the sums work.

Seed → 33 days under LEDs, trays moved by machine → 30,000 heads of lettuce a day, every day of the year

Proof: Keihanna runs at a 97% production yield — almost nothing it grows is thrown away (figures reported by Japan’s Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry, Kansai bureau).

Every ray of its light is bought as electricity. Spread survives where flashier rivals failed by automating the labour away and selling freshness and safety at Japanese prices.

Details

Spread was founded in Kyoto in 2006. At its Techno Farm Keihanna plant, opened in 2018, machines carry the growing trays from seeding to harvest: Japan’s industry ministry counts about 70% of the work automated, and about 110 millilitres of fresh water behind each head of lettuce — everything else is caught and used again. Spread’s first factory reached profit in 2013, a world first for a large indoor farm, and its newest, at Fukuroi, opened in 2024 at ten tonnes of lettuce a day.

Little Leaf Farms grows lettuce under glass, in real sunlight, with its roots in water instead of soil — cut one day, in stores the next.

Seed sown under glass → 24 days on moving gutters, drinking rainwater → cut and in stores within a day

Proof: a decade on from its first greenhouse, Farm Journal counts Little Leaf North America’s top producer of indoor-grown lettuce, with over half the market.

Its headline saving — up to 90% less water than field farming — is the company’s own figure, not independently audited, and “world’s largest” is the company’s own description of its Pennsylvania campus.

Details

Founder Paul Sellew, a Cornell horticulture graduate who had already built a greenhouse tomato company, opened the first greenhouse at Devens, Massachusetts in 2015. A second campus at McAdoo, Pennsylvania grew to forty acres of greenhouses by 2025, and a third site in Tennessee planted its first seeds in 2026. The crop is grown without chemical pesticides, herbicides or fungicides.

BrightFarms grows salad greens in greenhouses beside the cities that eat them, instead of trucking lettuce thousands of miles from California and Arizona.

Greenhouses beside the cities → greens cut near the customer → salad that travels hours, not cross-country days

Proof: its three 2024 hubs quadrupled its growing capacity in six months, and its parent, Cox Farms, is now North America’s largest greenhouse operator.

The rebuild had a cost. In February 2025 BrightFarms announced it would suspend operations at three of its earliest greenhouses — Selinsgrove, Pennsylvania, Rochelle, Illinois and Hendersonville, North Carolina — and concentrate on the new hubs; its New Hampshire and Virginia greenhouses stayed open.

Details

BrightFarms was founded in 2011 by Paul Lightfoot and Ted Caplow. Cox Enterprises took a majority stake in 2020 and bought the company outright in 2021 — the first full acquisition of an indoor-farming company. Cox then rebuilt BrightFarms around three regional hubs opened in 2024 — Yorkville, Illinois; Lorena, Texas; Macon, Georgia — each with eight acres under glass, growing some six million pounds of lettuce a year. Its greens sell in more than 6,500 stores.

In Boden, Swedish Lapland, a warehouse of Bitcoin miners works for Sweden’s grid first and mines second — powering up or down the moment the grid calls.

Too much power → the miners soak it up → too little → the miners switch off in about a second

Proof: the company counts 11,245 calls in one year, and says grid services now earn more than mining.

Every figure in this example is the company’s own.

Details

Flexionics Energy, a Swiss company, runs about 14 megawatts of miners at Boden and Utansjö — a draw big enough to matter to a grid, in machines that can change it at once. The sites sell balancing services to Svenska kraftnät, Sweden’s national grid operator: when wind and hydro power overshoot or fall short, the operator pays users who can shift their demand on the spot, a service called demand response. Flexionics says its machines answer in under a second. In the year covered by Joe Nakamoto’s film Inside Sweden’s Strangest Bitcoin Mine — a film Flexionics sponsored, and says so — 58% of income came from grid services rather than bitcoin. Even worn-out “Frankenstein” miners earn their keep here: flexibility does not need the newest machines.

Duke University’s Nicholas Institute, in its February 2025 report Rethinking Load Growth, calculates that the United States grid could add 76 gigawatts of new load — about a tenth of its peak demand — with no new power stations or grid upgrades, if the new load can switch off for up to 0.25% of the year (about 22 hours). The report is about flexible load in general, not Bitcoin; a mine is the rare load that meets its condition — it switches off in seconds, and nothing spoils.

Heatbit builds miners inside home appliances: a mining chip turns electricity into heat exactly as an electric heater does — and produces bitcoin on the way through.

Electricity → the chips do their sums → every watt ends up as heat in the water → hot showers and bitcoin from the same power

Proof: no prototype — Heatbit has shipped mining heaters since 2020.

The honest limit: a heat pump moves two to four times more heat per watt than any resistance heater — the trick beats ordinary electric heat, not a heat pump. Bitwater is a pre-order, due in early 2027 by the company’s schedule; the heaters and purifiers are in homes today.

Details

Heatbit, founded in 2020, builds the miner inside appliances a home already runs — heaters, air purifiers, and now a hot-water tank’s heating element. Its Bitwater element screws into an electric water tank in place of the lower element: twenty mining chips heat the water and mine at up to 34 TH/s (terahashes per second — the speed of the sums), easing off when the tank reaches its temperature. Its Bitair purifier cleans air through a HEPA filter, with a small miner running inside.

Six bitcoin servers stand among a Dutch grower’s tulip beds: their hot air grows the tulips, dries the bulbs, and cuts the gas bill.

Solar power from the roof → hot air off the servers → tulips warmed, bulbs dried → less gas burned, bitcoin earned

Proof: at work since 2022, still running in 2026 — Dutch reports that spring found the heat aimed at drying the harvested bulbs.

De Groot calls the operation carbon negative — his claim; no audited figures for the gas or carbon saved exist.

Details

The grower is Danielle Koning; the miner is Bert de Groot, founder of Bitcoin Brabant, who sells the farm’s tulips for bitcoin as Bitcoinbloem. Koning saw a mining video de Groot posted and called him. Her company owns half the servers and keeps their bitcoin; de Groot keeps his half for visits cleaning dust and insects from the fans. Each server cost €15,000; the greenhouse’s location stays secret at Koning’s request, against thieves. The air leaves about 20 degrees warmer than it enters, de Groot says. Koning puts it plainly: ‘The most important thing we get out of it is, we save on natural gas. Secondly, well, we earn Bitcoin.’ Dried bulbs resist mould and keep for export — by 2026 the heat was aimed at them.

In Finland’s Satakunta region, MARA built a mine whose hot water feeds the district heating network that warms the town.

Miners run → their heat is caught in water → town pipes carry it into homes → the town burns less boiler fuel

Proof: the 2-megawatt pilot, on since 2024, serves 11,000 people; MARA then grew its Finnish mining heat to about 80,000 residents.

The honest edges: it began as a pilot, testing whether selling heat can push a mine’s power cost towards zero, and the company has not published how much of each town’s heat the miners supply.

Details

District heating means water is heated in one place and piped through a town; most Finnish towns have one, much of it fed by burning wood. MARA, one of the world’s largest bitcoin miners, switched on the 2-megawatt site in June 2024, built to plug straight into that network — its first site designed for heating from the start.

Zengamina, a small hydro plant in north-west Zambia, powers some 15,000 people — yet for years over half its electricity went unsold, until a container of miners became its anchor customer.

River power nobody could buy → 120 miners in a container buy the surplus → income steadies → prices fall, more homes connect

Proof: within about a year, some 2,000 more people and 40 businesses had connected (Forbes).

Details

Gridless, founded in Nairobi in 2022 and backed by Block and Stillmark, parks containers of miners at small renewable plants across Kenya, Malawi and Zambia — six sites — as buyer of last resort: the customer who takes whatever power the neighbours do not. At Zengamina, mining brings in about 30% of the plant’s revenue, and the miners throttle down the moment homes, clinics and mills need the power. The plant itself was built with charity money in the 2000s to power a hospital, and the miners were never meant to be permanent. As local demand grows they matter less — and when Zengamina linked up with Zambia’s national grid, the surplus found a bigger buyer, with Gridless ready to move its container to the next plant that needs a first customer.

A gas flare at a well in the Bakken, North Dakota — the burning-off this example replaces. The photograph shows a flare, not one of the engine sites.

Oil wells produce gas they often cannot sell, so they burn it off in flares; since 2018, generators and miners at the wells have burned that gas in engines instead.

Stranded gas → a generator at the well burns it → the electricity mines bitcoin on the spot → the methane burns almost completely

Proof: measured flares destroy only about 91% of their methane; a gas engine destroys about 99.9%.

The counterweight: the carbon dioxide still enters the air, and paying for well gas can keep marginal wells alive longer.

Details

The honest version matters: burning the gas makes the same carbon dioxide in an engine as in a flare. What changes is the methane that escapes unburned — methane warms the planet roughly 80 times faster than carbon dioxide over twenty years. The 2022 study in Science measured American flares from the air and found them destroying about 91% of their methane, not the 98% long assumed; some were not lit at all. An engine, which must burn cleanly to keep running, destroys about 99.9%. Crusoe pioneered the pattern, then sold the business to NYDIG in 2025; it says its 425 units kept 22 billion cubic feet of gas out of flares and cut the gas’s warming impact by about 63% — its own figures.

Learn more: Plant et al. in Science, 2022 — the flare study ↗

A flare burning methane at a landfill — the fate this example replaces. The photograph shows a landfill flare, not the Utah site.

At the Davis Landfill in Layton, Utah, Nodal Power burns the landfill’s methane in an engine and mines bitcoin with the electricity — entirely off-grid.

Landfill gas with no buyer → an engine at the landfill burns it → the electricity mines bitcoin on the spot → the methane is destroyed and the landfill is paid

Proof: the companies published the site’s first 240 days of operation — 92% uptime, and power at 3 cents per kilowatt-hour against an industrial average of 8.

The honest edges: the site is a 270-kilowatt pilot, small next to the grid-scale mines, and every figure comes from the companies’ own report.

Details

Landfill gas is about half methane, rising out of buried rubbish for decades — and at most landfills it is burned off in a flare or let escape, too weak and too variable to clean up for a pipeline. Nodal Power, a Utah company, has run bitcoin miners on that gas since 2022; MARA, one of the world’s largest bitcoin miners, joined the site in September 2023. In the published first 240 days, September 2023 to May 2024, the site used about 16.1 million cubic feet of methane instead of releasing it, while the landfill earned revenue from gas it could not otherwise sell.

Nature as Model, Measure, and Mentor

What gets copied is the principle, never just the shape — the cases below each put one principle to work, in a bullet train, a fastener, and a film copied from a shark’s skin.

Biologist Janine Benyus named the practice in her 1997 book Biomimicry: Innovation Inspired by Nature. Her point: life has tested its designs for 3.8 billion years, and every species alive today is a design that passed the test. The Biomimicry Institute, the non-profit Benyus co-founded, runs AskNature — a free library that catalogues nature’s strategies by the job each strategy does (moving water, gripping a surface, staying cool), so a designer facing the same job can start from life’s answer.

The kingfisherThe train

A train entering a tunnel at speed squeezes the air ahead of it into a wave that bursts out the far end as a boom; the fix was copied from a kingfisher’s beak.

Kingfisher slips in splash-free → its beak’s shape wins the tests → the tunnel boom fades

Proof: the redesigned train ran 10% faster than the one before it, on about 15% less electricity — inside the noise limits.

Details

The boom rattled houses hundreds of metres from the tunnel mouth. Engineer Eiji Nakatsu, a keen birdwatcher, thought of the kingfisher, which dives from air into water without a splash, on a long beak that widens only gradually. His team tested nose shapes by firing models down a pipe; the shape that made the smallest wave was almost identical to the beak. The 500 Series entered service in 1997 with a 15-metre nose — half its front car.

The burrThe fastener

Swiss engineer George de Mestral came home from an Alpine walk in 1941 with burdock burrs stuck to his trousers and his dog’s fur — and put one under a microscope.

Burr’s hooks catch loops → hooks and loops woven in nylon → a fastener that grips, peels, and grips again

Proof: NASA put it everywhere — Apollo 11 alone carried some 3,300 square inches of the fastener to hold tools and equipment down in weightlessness.

Details

Under the microscope he saw why the burrs held on: each burr is covered in tiny hooks, and the hooks had caught in the loops of cloth and fur. He set out to copy it — one strip carrying thousands of small hooks, the other thousands of soft loops. It took him more than a decade: the patent was granted in 1954, and the name joins the French velours (velvet) and crochet (hook).

Learn more: AskNature ↗

The footThe copy

A gecko walks up glass and hangs from ceilings — no glue, no suction — using millions of tiny toe hairs that space robots now copy.

Toe hairs grip by molecular pull → copied in rubber → grippers stick, hold, release

Proof: NASA has flown gecko grippers on the space station since 2016.

Details

How the grip works. Each toe hair is called a seta (say “SEE-tee”), and every seta ends in hundreds of finer tips. Pressed against a surface, the tips come so close that the faint pull between molecules — van der Waals forces — adds up over millions of contacts into one powerful grip. Nothing is sticky, and nothing is left behind.

One-way, and self-cleaning. The grip points one way. Drag a toe along a surface and the hairs lock on; roll the toe upward and they peel free — that rolling peel is how a gecko takes each step. The hairs are keratin, the stuff of fingernails, packed about five thousand to a square millimetre of toe, and the feet clean themselves as they walk: dirt clings to the wall more strongly than to the hairs, so a few steps shed it.

Why space wants it. The pull needs no air, so it holds in a vacuum — where ordinary glue would freeze, dry out, or poison the cabin air.

The latest test, and the copy on sale. In 2024 an Astrobee robot on the space station was put through its paces with gecko-pad gripping arms (the REACCH test), practising catching satellites. The copy is also on sale: geCKo Materials, a California company grown out of Stanford research, sells reusable gecko-style tiles for factory robots and spacecraft — the company rates a one-inch tile to hold 15 pounds through more than 120,000 stick-and-release cycles.

Learn more: AskNature ↗

The moundThe building

Harare’s Eastgate Centre was built with no air conditioning at all — its cooling is copied from a termite mound that holds its temperature within about a degree, day and night.

Termites steer air through mud → the building breathes the same way → cool rooms, no air conditioning

Proof: Eastgate’s ventilation uses about a tenth of the energy of a conventionally cooled building’s, and its measured total energy ran 35% below six comparable Harare offices.

Later research suggests real mounds breathe more like lungs than chimneys — the building’s measured savings stand either way.

Details

The termite Macrotermes michaelseni farms a fungus in its mound that thrives only within about a degree of 30°C, while outside air swings from near freezing at night to over 40°C by day. The mound manages it with no machinery: the colony opens and closes tunnels to steer air through mud that soaks up the day’s heat and gives it back at night. Architect Mick Pearce, with engineers Arup, copied the arrangement for the Eastgate Centre (1996): low-power fans pull cool night air through the building’s concrete mass, and 48 brick chimneys let the day’s warmed air escape — no air-conditioning plant was ever bought, saving some $3.5 million.

Learn more: AskNature ↗

The spiderThe strand

A spider spins her silk from liquid protein at body temperature — no pressure, no acid — pulling it solid as she draws it out.

Spider spins it → scientists unravel how → labs brew the protein

Proof: her dragline absorbs three times Kevlar’s energy before breaking — it stretches instead of snapping.

The copy still trails: Spiber and Bolt Threads, its best-funded imitators, have collapsed; AMSilk survives, still small; its sales came in runs of about fifty.

Details

Her silk starts as liquid protein dissolved in water. She never pushes it out — she pulls it: drawn through a narrowing passage, the liquid loses its water and locks solid. A separate gland makes each silk — stiff for the frame, stretchy for the catching spiral. Kevlar, the bulletproof-vest fibre, is made the opposite way: petroleum chemicals dissolved in concentrated sulfuric acid, squirted through fine holes to set.

Learn more: AskNature ↗

The shark’s skinThe film

A shark’s skin is tiled with tiny grooved teeth that keep water sliding close and orderly over the body — the copy is a riblet film that now flies on airliners.

Denticle surface → copied at micro-scale → a riblet film flying on airliners

Proof: in 1990s flight tests, riblet films covering most of a test plane cut its total drag by about 3%.

The famous swimsuit copy is the doubtful one — in controlled tests its fabric showed no consistent speed gain; plain ribbed surfaces are where the measured cuts are.

Details

The skin’s tiny tooth-like scales are called denticles, each ribbed with fine grooves running with the flow. The grooves keep water sliding close instead of breaking away into drag-making swirls: in tank tests, ribbed surfaces have cut drag by up to about 10%. The same texture leaves algae and barnacles nothing to grip, so the surface stays clean as well as quick. The film flying today is AeroSHARK, from Lufthansa Technik and BASF, covered in riblets 50 micrometres high — half the thickness of a human hair. Airlines have flown the film on Boeing 777s since October 2022, and about 950 square metres of the film on one 777 cuts the plane’s total fuel burn by about 1%, saving roughly 300 to 400 tonnes of fuel per plane each year.

Renault has rebuilt worn engines and gearboxes outside Paris since 1949 — a rebuilt unit sells with the same guarantee as a new one, for 30 to 50% less.

Worn engine → stripped and rebuilt to new spec → sold again in place of a new one

Products Designed as Nutrients

Waste = food. In nature nothing is thrown away — what one living thing leaves behind feeds another. Cradle to Cradle designs products the same way: every material is food for one of two cycles, and which cycle is decided before the product is made.

Biological nutrients — food for the soil

Products of consumption — things that get used up, like food packaging and natural fibres. Their materials are safe to rot: after use they decompose, become soil, and feed new growth.

Technical nutrients — food for industry

Products of service — things used but not used up, like a chair, a carpet, a machine part. Their metals and high-quality materials are returned, taken apart and remade at the same quality — never downcycled into lesser goods, and never leaked into the soil.

The model is the book’s cherry tree: it makes thousands of blossoms, far more than it needs, and none of it is waste — what falls decomposes and feeds the soil, the insects and the birds. Not efficient. Effective.

The book states the method as three principles: waste = food; use current solar income — run on today’s sunlight, not stored carbon; and celebrate diversity — design that fits its place.

The idea comes from William McDonough and Michael Braungart’s 2002 book Cradle to Cradle: Remaking the Way We Make Things. The certificate is a separate thing, run by the non-profit Cradle to Cradle Products Innovation Institute, which the two authors founded: the Institute scores products and materials against the book’s principles under the current standard, Version 5.0 — and it does not certify buildings. A certificate runs three years before the product is assessed again, and certified products can contribute toward LEED credits.

The Empire State Building’s retrofit treated the 1931 tower as a machine to be tuned, not a relic to be replaced. Its most copied move was the windows: all 6,514 were rebuilt on site, in a workshop set up inside the building — each pane taken out, fitted with an insulating film and a sealed gas fill, and set back in its original frame. Over 96% of the old glass and frames stayed in service. Rebuilt chillers, insulated radiators and modern controls did the rest, and the tower earned LEED Gold in 2011.

Landmark tower, dated systems → deep retrofit in place, windows rebuilt on site → the same tower, using a third less energy

Proof: energy use fell by 38%, saving about $4.4 million a year — the extra efficiency work paid for itself in roughly three years.

Phipps Conservatory’s Center for Sustainable Landscapes in Pittsburgh, opened in 2012, was designed to behave like the plants it houses. It makes all its own energy, from solar panels and a vertical-axis wind turbine, and it captures and treats all the water that falls on or flows through the site, in lagoons and constructed wetlands. It uses about 70% less energy than a typical office building — and has not paid an electric bill in over a decade.

Passive House — Passivhaus in German — is a building standard run by the Passive House Institute in Darmstadt, Germany. There are no points to trade, so solar panels cannot make up for a leaky wall. Designers prove the numbers before building, with the Institute’s Passive House Planning Package software, and an accredited certifier checks the finished building; the Institute counts more than 55,300 certified Passive House units worldwide, totalling 5 million square metres of floor area, as of July 2026.

In 1991, in Darmstadt’s Kranichstein district, four families moved into a row of terraced houses built on an idea physicist Wolfgang Feist had worked out with the Swedish professor Bo Adamson: wrap a house so well — thick insulation, airtight joints, windows that hold heat in — that the little warmth it still needs can arrive in the fresh air the ventilation brings in anyway. No radiators, no furnace. The builders buried hundreds of sensors in the fabric, to check the claim instead of trusting it.

Heat held in by insulation and airtightness → the last warmth rides in on the fresh air → no heating system to speak of

Proof: twenty-five years on, experts re-examined everything from the fabric to the ventilation ducts; the Passive House Institute’s verdict: the first Passive House still works as planned.

Every other Antarctic research station runs on diesel shipped in. Belgium’s Princess Elisabeth station, opened in 2009 on a granite ridge in East Antarctica, runs on what the place itself provides: nine wind turbines supply about 70% of its electricity, solar panels cover the roof and walls for most of the rest, and a bank of batteries stores the surplus; a smart grid hands out power in order of need. Melted snow is treated and reused. Built to the Passive House standard, it keeps what little heat it makes.

Wind and sun at the pole → batteries and a smart grid share them out → a warm station, no fuel to ship in

Proof: it is still the only research station in Antarctica running entirely on renewable energy — and through the polar winter it stands empty, kept alive on stored power and watched from Belgium.

Regenerative Building Standard

What it is

The Living Building Challenge asks a building to work like a flower: gather its own sunlight and water, waste nothing, and give back more than it takes. It is run by the International Living Future Institute, a non-profit, and has been running since 2006.

The Institute’s model is a flower with seven Petals — the seven areas a building must get right: Place, Water, Energy, Health + Happiness, Materials, Equity and Beauty. Between them the Petals hold 20 Imperatives — the individual must-do requirements.

Why it works

No building passes on drawings. Before any certificate, the Institute audits 12 straight months of the building’s real, measured performance — a full year of proof that it actually lives this way.

What it asks of a building

The full standard — called Living — means all 20 Imperatives. Two shorter paths exist: Core means the 10 Imperatives marked ★, the ten every path requires; Petal certification means those 10 Core plus every Imperative in one whole Petal — Water, Energy or Materials.

Details — the 20 Imperatives

What a Living Building must actually do, grouped by Petal. ★ marks a Core Imperative — one of the ten every certification path requires.

Place · 4 Imperatives — restoring a healthy interrelationship with nature.
  • 01 Ecology of Place ★ — Protect and restore the land the building sits on; never build on wild or prime farm land.
  • 02 Urban Agriculture — Grow food on part of the site.
  • 03 Habitat Exchange — Protect an equal area of wild land somewhere else, permanently.
  • 04 Human-Scaled Living ★ — Build compact and walkable, sized for people, not cars.
Water · 2 Imperatives — developments that operate within the water balance of a given place and climate.
  • 05 Responsible Water Use ★ — Use far less drinking water than a typical building.
  • 06 Net Positive Water — Catch, treat and reuse all the water the building needs, on its own site.
Energy · 2 Imperatives — relying only on current solar income.
  • 07 Energy + Carbon Reduction ★ — Use far less energy than a typical building, and cut carbon with it.
  • 08 Net Positive Carbon — Make more renewable energy on site than the building uses, and offset the carbon spent building it.
Health + Happiness · 3 Imperatives — environments that optimize physical and psychological health and well-being.
  • 09 Healthy Interior Environment ★ — Fresh air, daylight and a view out for everyone inside.
  • 10 Healthy Interior Performance — Test the indoor air after move-in, and keep it clean.
  • 11 Access to Nature — Put nature within everyone’s reach, inside and out.
Materials · 5 Imperatives — endorsing products that are safe for all species through time.
  • 12 Responsible Materials ★ — Choose products whose ingredients are openly disclosed.
  • 13 Red List — Use none of the worst toxic chemicals on the Institute’s banned list.
  • 14 Responsible Sourcing — Buy wood and materials from certified, responsible sources.
  • 15 Living Economy Sourcing — Buy from makers and suppliers near the site.
  • 16 Net Positive Waste — Send almost nothing to landfill, in building or in use.
Equity · 2 Imperatives — supporting a just and equitable world.
  • 17 Universal Access ★ — Design so everyone can enter, use and enjoy the building.
  • 18 Inclusion ★ — Hire diverse businesses and share the project’s benefits with the community.
Beauty · 2 Imperatives — celebrating design that uplifts the human spirit.
  • 19 Beauty + Biophilia ★ — Make the building beautiful, with nature woven through it.
  • 20 Education + Inspiration ★ — Show how the building works, and open it to the public.
