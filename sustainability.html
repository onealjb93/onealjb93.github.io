Sustainable Development

Sustainability is not perfection — it is improvement.

Applied frameworks and systems thinking integrating economic progress, environmental preservation, and structural equity.

Underneath it all is one idea: bend the straight line — take, make, use, waste — into a loop, where what comes back feeds what comes next.

Loess Plateau, China

Example — Landscape Restoration: The Loess Plateau in China was one of the most eroded places on Earth, its bare hills shedding soil into the Yellow River. Between 1994 and 2005, World Bank-backed projects helped farmers terrace the slopes, plant the hills with trees and grass, and dam the gullies — across some 35,000 square kilometres. In the project areas grain harvests rose by more than half, the sediment flowing toward the Yellow River fell by an estimated 110 million tonnes a year, and 2.5 million people rose out of poverty.

Learn more: World Bank project ↗

From a Line to a Loop

Most of the economy still runs in a straight line. Sustainability means bending that line into a loop — here is the whole idea in one picture:

The line

Resources flow in one end and out the other. The last box is a dead end — everything taken ends up thrown away.

The loop

Design the waste out from the start, and what comes back — repaired, reused, returned — feeds what comes next. Nothing has to be thrown away.

Sustainability is not perfection — it is improvement. Bend the line where you can: one exchange, one repair, one return at a time. Don't make perfect the enemy of the good.

Money is a layer too. A currency designed to lose value rewards the line — spend now, replace often. A money built to hold its value rewards the loop. The case for Bitcoin as sustainable money is on the Bitcoin page.

In Kalundborg, a town in Denmark, the factories have spent decades learning to feed each other. The Asnæs power station, an oil refinery, and the medicine plants trade steam, gas, water, and leftovers through a web they call an industrial symbiosis — and it is already a loop, because what one plant throws off is what the next one runs on:

Steam & Gas

The power station's steam runs the refinery and the medicine plants, and its leftover heat warms the town's homes. The refinery's surplus gas flows back the other way, to be burned as fuel.

Circulated
Gypsum & Ash

Cleaning the power station's flue gas makes gypsum, which Gyproc turns into plasterboard instead of mining it. The ash left from burning is used to make cement.

Built In
Water & Sludge

The refinery's treated wastewater and used cooling water are piped to the power station and used again. The sludge left over from making medicine is spread on nearby farms as fertilizer.

Returned

Two more stories

California Academy of Sciences, San Francisco

Example: The California Academy of Sciences, San Francisco (Renzo Piano, 2008): a natural-history museum under a living hill — two and a half acres of native wildflowers rolling over the building’s two domes, insulating everything beneath. It was among the first LEED Platinum museum buildings, certified by the U.S. Green Building Council, and is still the emblematic one.

Learn more: California Academy of Sciences ↗

Joel Salatin’s Polyface Farm

Example: Joel Salatin’s Polyface Farm: the herd grazes one small paddock for a single day, then moves on; the chickens follow in mobile shelters, scratching through the manure, spreading it and fertilizing the pasture behind the cattle. The photographs set the two systems side by side: Polyface’s herd on living pasture, and the industrial alternative — cattle finished in a bare-dirt feedlot. Pigs work the woodland in his “pigerator” system. Salatin laid the model out in his own books — Salad Bar Beef for the grazing, Pastured Poultry Profits for the pastured poultry — and Michael Pollan profiled the farm at length in The Omnivore’s Dilemma (2006).

Learn more: Polyface Farm ↗

More Loops at Work

Three more places where the loop already runs — a country, a city's water, and a port's exhaust:

Costa Rica

The fire is not a metaphor. Costa Rica's forest was burned and cleared for cattle pasture until, by the 1980s, only about a fifth of the country was still forest. The change since is just as real: the country began paying landowners to protect and replant, and forest cover has climbed back past half.

Example — Paying for Ecosystem Services: By 1987, after decades of clearing, only about 21% of Costa Rica was still forest. In 1997 the country began paying landowners to keep trees standing and let them grow back — the PSA programme, run by the national forestry fund FONAFIFO and paid for largely by a small tax on fossil fuels. Forest cover has since climbed back to more than half the country, making Costa Rica one of the few countries in the world to reverse deforestation.

Learn more: FONAFIFO ↗

NEWater, Singapore

Example — The Water Loop: Singapore has almost no fresh water of its own, so it loops the water it uses. Used water from every sink and toilet is collected and treated, then pushed through microfiltration, reverse osmosis, and ultraviolet light — and comes out cleaner than drinking-water standards demand. NEWater now meets up to 40% of the city-state's water demand, and is planned to reach 55% by 2060.

Learn more: PUB — NEWater ↗

OCAP CO₂ Pipeline, Rotterdam

Both photographs are real: on the left is Shell's Pernis refinery, one of Europe's largest, whose waste CO₂ once went straight up those stacks. Since 2005 a pipeline has carried it instead to the Westland greenhouses on the right, where it feeds tomatoes, peppers and flowers.

Example — Industrial Symbiosis: Greenhouse plants grow faster in CO₂-rich air, so Dutch growers used to burn natural gas just to make CO₂. Since 2005, OCAP has instead captured the CO₂ that Rotterdam's industry would have vented — starting with Shell's Pernis refinery — and piped it to the greenhouses of Westland and beyond. It now delivers hundreds of thousands of tonnes a year to more than 600 growers: the refinery's waste becomes the plants' food, and growers burn far less gas.

Learn more: OCAP ↗

Curitiba, Brazil

In the 1970s Curitiba planned for a city its people could afford. A subway was beyond its means, so in 1974 it opened the world’s first bus rapid transit: buses running in their own lanes, boarded at tube stations where riders pay before the bus arrives, all for one flat fare. Parks were laid out on flood-prone land too wet to build on, where they hold floodwater instead of letting it drown homes. And through the Green Exchange, residents of the poorest districts trade bags of collected trash for food and bus tokens.

Trash collected → swapped for food and bus tokens → cleaner streets, fed families, buses full

Proof: more than 70% of the city’s commuters ride the bus — and about 70% of its garbage is recycled.

Gabe Brown's Ranch, North Dakota

Gabe Brown farms some 5,000 acres near Bismarck, North Dakota. Hail wiped out his crops in 1995, and again in 1998, and the losses pushed him to stop fighting his soil and start feeding it: no ploughing, a changing mix of cover crops on every field, and cattle moved through in tight herds, the way bison once crossed the prairie. The soil answered. Rain that once soaked in at half an inch an hour now soaks in at up to eight inches an hour.

Worn soil → cover crops, no-till, cattle → organic matter from under 2% to roughly 5–8% → rain soaks in instead of running off

Proof: the untouched prairie nearby holds 7–8% organic matter; Brown’s fields, once under 2%, are closing on the soil that was there before farming.

Learn more: Brown's Ranch ↗

The Honeynut Squash

In 2009, chef Dan Barber put a challenge to Cornell plant breeder Michael Mazourek: breed a butternut squash that actually tastes good. The supermarket butternut is bred big, uniform and hard to bruise — and comes out watery and bland. Working from a butternut–buttercup cross first made at Cornell in the 1980s, Mazourek selected plant by plant for flavour, with Barber tasting as he went. The result broke the supermarket brief: half the size, thin edible skin, flesh so concentrated it needs no sweetening — and two to three times a butternut’s beta-carotene.

Chef asks for flavour → breeder selects for taste, not size → a smaller, sweeter squash people seek out

Proof: the squash started a company — in 2018 Barber and Mazourek co-founded Row 7 Seed Company, breeding vegetables for flavour first.

Learn more: Row 7 Seed Company ↗

KickStart MoneyMaker Pumps, Kenya

KickStart’s MoneyMaker pumps are worked like a small stair-stepper: standing on two treadles, a farmer draws water from as deep as seven metres — a stream, a well, a pond — and spreads it over fields that would otherwise sit dry until the rains. The Super MoneyMaker sells for about $100, real money in rural Kenya. It is sold, never donated, on purpose: a pump a farmer has paid for gets used, repaired and resold. What it buys is a second harvest — crops grown in the dry season, when prices are highest.

Pump sold, not donated → farmer irrigates in the dry season → a second growing season every year

Proof: more than 370,000 pumps are now in use, and the Skoll Foundation counts 1.4 million people lifted a major step out of poverty.

Learn more: KickStart International ↗

500 Series Shinkansen, Japan

A train entering a tunnel at speed squeezes the air ahead of it into a pressure wave that races down the tunnel and bursts out the far end as a boom that rattled houses hundreds of metres away. Engineer Eiji Nakatsu, a keen birdwatcher, thought of the kingfisher, which dives from air into water without a splash, on a long beak that widens only gradually. His team tested nose shapes by firing models down a pipe; the shape that made the smallest wave was almost identical to the beak. The 500 Series entered service in 1997 with a 15-metre nose — half its front car.

Kingfisher slips in splash-free → its beak’s shape wins the tests → the tunnel boom fades

Proof: the redesigned train ran 10% faster than the one before it, on about 15% less electricity — inside the noise limits.

Learn more: AskNature ↗

Velcro

In 1941, Swiss engineer George de Mestral came home from a walk in the Alps with burdock burrs stuck to his trousers and his dog’s fur. Under a microscope he saw why they held on: each burr is covered in tiny hooks, and the hooks had caught in the loops of cloth and fur. He set out to copy it — one strip carrying thousands of small hooks, the other thousands of soft loops. It took him more than a decade: the patent was granted in 1954, and the name joins the French velours (velvet) and crochet (hook).

Burr’s hooks catch loops → hooks and loops woven in nylon → a fastener that grips, peels, and grips again

Proof: NASA put it everywhere — Apollo 11 alone carried some 3,300 square inches of the fastener to hold tools and equipment down in weightlessness.

Learn more: AskNature ↗

The Lotus Effect

The leaf is rough twice over — tiny bumps, each coated in tinier wax crystals. A raindrop rests on the bump tips, barely touching the leaf, so it stays a ball and rolls off at the slightest tilt. Dirt sits loosely on the same tips, and sticks to a rolling drop better than to the leaf — so each drop picks it up and carries it away.

A painted wall had no such trick: grime meant scrubbing with harsh chemicals and repainting again over the years. Sto’s Lotusan paint, on sale since 1999, copies the leaf’s rough finish onto walls, so the rain washes them instead.

Lotus leaf stays clean → Barthlott finds the bumps-and-wax trick → paint copies the roughness

Proof: since 1999, Lotusan has coated some 300,000 buildings.

Learn more: AskNature ↗

Eastgate Centre, Harare

The termite Macrotermes michaelseni farms a fungus in its mound that thrives only within about a degree of 30°C, while outside air swings from near freezing at night to over 40°C by day. The mound manages it with no machinery: the colony opens and closes tunnels to steer air through mud that soaks up the day’s heat and gives it back at night. Architect Mick Pearce, with engineers Arup, copied the arrangement for Harare’s Eastgate Centre (1996): low-power fans pull cool night air through the building’s concrete mass, and 48 brick chimneys let the day’s warmed air escape — no air-conditioning plant was ever bought, saving some $3.5 million.

Termites steer air through mud → the building breathes the same way → cool rooms, no air conditioning

Proof: Eastgate’s ventilation uses about a tenth of the energy of a conventionally cooled building’s, and its measured total energy ran 35% below six comparable Harare offices.

Later research suggests real mounds breathe more like lungs than chimneys — the building’s measured savings stand either way.

Learn more: AskNature ↗

Spider Silk

Her silk starts as liquid protein dissolved in water. She never pushes it out — she pulls it: drawn through a narrowing passage, the liquid loses its water and locks solid, at body temperature, no pressure, no acid. Kevlar, the bulletproof-vest fibre, is made the opposite way: petroleum chemicals dissolved in concentrated sulfuric acid, squirted through fine holes to set. A separate gland makes each silk — stiff for the frame, stretchy for the catching spiral.

Spider spins it → scientists unravel how → labs brew the protein

Proof: her dragline absorbs three times Kevlar’s energy before breaking — it stretches instead of snapping.

The copy still trails: Spiber and Bolt Threads, its best-funded imitators, have collapsed; AMSilk survives, still small; its sales came in runs of about fifty.

Learn more: AskNature ↗

Shark Skin

A shark’s skin is tiled with tiny tooth-like scales — denticles — each ribbed with fine grooves running with the flow. The grooves keep water sliding close and orderly over the body instead of breaking away into drag-making swirls: in tank tests, ribbed surfaces have cut drag by up to about 10%. The same texture leaves algae and barnacles nothing to grip, so the surface stays clean as well as quick.

Denticle surface → copied at micro-scale → racing suits, films and coatings

Proof: riblet films copied from the skin have cut an airliner’s total drag by about 3% in flight tests.

The famous swimsuit copy is the doubtful one — in controlled tests its fabric showed no consistent speed gain; plain ribbed surfaces are where the measured cuts are.

Learn more: AskNature ↗

Fairphone, Amsterdam

Most phones are glued shut, so a cracked screen or a tired battery means a new phone. Fairphone builds the opposite way: the phone comes apart into modules — screen, battery, camera and more — held by ordinary screws, and the owner swaps a broken part at the kitchen table with a standard screwdriver. The Fairphone 5, launched in 2023, has ten replaceable modules, a five-year warranty, and software support promised until 2031.

Part breaks → owner swaps the module → the phone stays in use for years

Proof: iFixit, the repair-guide publisher, scores the Fairphone 5 a perfect 10 out of 10 for repairability.

Learn more: Fairphone ↗

Renault, Choisy-le-Roi

Renault has rebuilt worn engines and gearboxes at Choisy-le-Roi, outside Paris, since 1949. A worn unit comes back, is stripped and cleaned, and every part is tested: what still measures true goes back in — a rebuilt gearbox leaves with about three-quarters of its parts pre-used — and the rest is replaced new, so the finished unit sells with the same guarantee as a new one, for 30 to 50% less. In 2021, after 71 years, the work moved to Renault’s Re-Factory at Flins, built to run the same loop at larger scale.

Worn engine → stripped and rebuilt to new spec → sold again in place of a new one

Proof: on the Ellen MacArthur Foundation’s figures, a remanufactured part typically uses 80% less energy, 88% less water and 70% less waste than making it new.

Learn more: Renault Group ↗

Caterpillar Reman

Caterpillar builds its heavy-machine parts to be used twice. Wearing surfaces are cast with extra material, so a worn engine or transmission can be machined back to its original measurements instead of melted down. The return is organised, not hoped for: whoever buys a rebuilt part pays a deposit on the old one — the core charge — and gets it back only when the worn part comes in. Cat Reman has run on that loop since 1973.

Worn part comes back for its deposit → stripped and machined to spec → sold again with a new part’s warranty

Proof: more than 8,000 Reman parts are on the shelf, at 45–85% of the new price when the old core is returned.

Learn more: Cat Reman ↗

Michelin Fleet Solutions

On Michelin’s per-kilometre contracts, the fleet never buys the tires: it pays for the distance its trucks travel, and the tires stay Michelin’s property and Michelin’s problem. That flips the maker’s incentive — every extra kilometre drawn from a casing is money earned — so Michelin’s technicians regroove worn tread, turn tires on the rim to even the wear, and retread sound casings with a fresh band of rubber. A Michelin truck tire is built with extra tread rubber for exactly this: one casing can live four lives — new, regrooved, retreaded, regrooved again.

Tire wears → Michelin regrooves or retreads it → back on the road → the fleet pays per kilometre, not per tire

Proof: more than 300,000 commercial vehicles in Europe run on these mileage contracts.

Learn more: Michelin Fleet Solutions ↗

Interface Carpet Tiles

In 1994, Interface’s founder Ray Anderson was asked what his carpet company was doing for the environment, and had no answer — he later called the moment a spear in the chest. The company he had built on oil-based nylon set itself Mission Zero: no negative impact on the environment by 2020. Its ReEntry programme takes worn tiles back and splits face from backing, so old carpet becomes new carpet; much of its yarn is now regenerated nylon, spun from discarded fishing nets and old carpet fibre.

Worn tiles → taken back → recycled → new tiles on the floor

Proof: in November 2019 Interface declared Mission Zero achieved, a year early — and every product it sells is carbon neutral across its full life.

Learn more: Interface ReEntry ↗

HP Ink Cartridges

HP’s Planet Partners programme has taken back empty ink and toner cartridges for more than 25 years. The loop is closed in the strict sense: returned cartridges are pulled apart, their plastic is cleaned and reprocessed, and that plastic — topped up with plastic from collected bottles and clothes hangers — is moulded into new HP cartridges, not downcycled into lesser goods. Since 2016 HP has also bought ocean-bound plastic collected by workers in Haiti, creating steadier local incomes as it keeps bottles out of the sea.

Empty cartridge → dropped off → material recovered → new cartridge

Proof: more than 875 million cartridges have been recycled through the programme — and HP says not one returned through it ends up in landfill.

Learn more: HP Planet Partners ↗

Empire State Building, New York

The Empire State Building’s retrofit treated the 1931 tower as a machine to be tuned, not a relic to be replaced. Its most copied move was the windows: all 6,514 were rebuilt on site, in a workshop set up inside the building — each pane taken out, fitted with an insulating film and a sealed gas fill, and set back in its original frame. Over 96% of the old glass and frames stayed in service. Rebuilt chillers, insulated radiators and modern controls did the rest, and the tower earned LEED Gold in 2011.

Landmark tower, dated systems → deep retrofit in place, windows rebuilt on site → the same tower, using a third less energy

Proof: energy use fell by 38%, saving about $4.4 million a year — the extra efficiency work paid for itself in roughly three years.

Learn more: Empire State Building ↗

Bullitt Center, Seattle

Seattle’s Bullitt Center, opened in 2013, was built to the Living Building Challenge — the standard that asks a building to run like a flower, gathering its own energy and water and wasting nothing; its founders chose it over LEED as the harder test. The roof is a canopy of 575 solar panels overhanging the building like a hat brim. Every drop of water comes from rain, held in a 56,000-gallon cistern and treated to drinking standard. The building uses about a fifth of the energy of a typical Seattle office.

Sun and rain fall on the building → panels and cistern catch them → the building runs on what it catches

Proof: across its certification year, the building produced 90,793 kWh more electricity than it used.

One system did not last: the composting toilets were replaced in 2021 by a vacuum system.

Learn more: Bullitt Center ↗ · Living Building Challenge ↗

Phipps Center for Sustainable Landscapes

Phipps Conservatory’s Center for Sustainable Landscapes in Pittsburgh, opened in 2012, was designed to behave like the plants it houses. It makes all its own energy, from solar panels and a vertical-axis wind turbine, and it captures and treats all the water that falls on or flows through the site, in lagoons and constructed wetlands. It uses about 70% less energy than a typical office building — and has not paid an electric bill in over a decade.

Rain falls on the site → lagoons and wetlands clean it → the building uses it again → even a ten-year storm sends nothing to the city sewer

Proof: it remains the only building in the world holding seven of the highest green certifications at once — among them the Living Building Challenge, LEED Platinum and SITES Platinum.

Learn more: Phipps Conservatory ↗ · Living Building Challenge ↗

The First Passive House, Darmstadt

In 1991, in Darmstadt’s Kranichstein district, four families moved into a row of terraced houses built on an idea physicist Wolfgang Feist had worked out with the Swedish professor Bo Adamson: wrap a house so well — thick insulation, airtight joints, windows that hold heat in — that the little warmth it still needs can arrive in the fresh air the ventilation brings in anyway. No radiators, no furnace. The builders buried hundreds of sensors in the fabric, to check the claim instead of trusting it.

Heat held in by insulation and airtightness → the last warmth rides in on the fresh air → no heating system to speak of

Proof: twenty-five years on, experts re-examined everything from the fabric to the ventilation ducts; the Passive House Institute’s verdict: the first Passive House still works as planned.

Learn more: Passivhaus Institut ↗

Princess Elisabeth Antarctica

Every other Antarctic research station runs on diesel shipped in. Belgium’s Princess Elisabeth station, opened in 2009 on a granite ridge in East Antarctica, runs on what the place itself provides: nine wind turbines supply about 70% of its electricity, solar panels cover the roof and walls for most of the rest, and a bank of batteries stores the surplus; a smart grid hands out power in order of need. Melted snow is treated and reused. Built to the Passive House standard, it keeps what little heat it makes.

Wind and sun at the pole → batteries and a smart grid share them out → a warm station, no fuel to ship in

Proof: it is still the only research station in Antarctica running entirely on renewable energy — and through the polar winter it stands empty, kept alive on stored power and watched from Belgium.

Learn more: Princess Elisabeth Antarctica ↗

Bahnstadt, Heidelberg

Heidelberg took its old freight railway yard — 116 hectares of sidings and sheds behind the main station — and set one rule for everything built on it: every building must meet the Passive House standard, and prove it in the standard’s own planning software before it earns a building permit. Homes, offices, laboratories, the school, the cinema — no exceptions, and no single showcase block: the almost-no-heating standard is simply the district’s building code. District heat comes from a wood-chip power plant.

Freight yard emptied → one rule for every new building → a whole district that barely needs heating

Proof: homes built so far emit about 0.13 tonnes of CO₂ per person per year for energy — the Heidelberg average is 2.0 tonnes.

Learn more: Bahnstadt Heidelberg ↗

Ford River Rouge, Dearborn

In 1999 Ford commissioned William McDonough — co-author of Cradle to Cradle — to remake the River Rouge complex, Henry Ford’s vast car plant in Dearborn. The new Dearborn Truck Plant wears a living roof of sedum across 10.4 acres, then the largest in the world, which soaks up rain where it falls. What runs off crosses porous paving, planted swales and wetlands, taking about two days to filter through to the Rouge River, cleaned on the way; runoff from the old hard site reached the river in minutes.

Rain lands on the roof → soil and plants slow it and clean it → the river receives it two days later

Proof: Ford estimated a conventional treatment plant at $50 million; the natural system cost less than a third of that, and cuts the solids washed off the site by at least 85%.
