Sustainable Development

Applied frameworks and systems thinking integrating economic progress, environmental preservation, and structural equity.

From a Line to a Loop

Most of the economy still runs in a straight line. Sustainability means bending that line into a loop — here is the whole idea in one picture:

The line

Resources flow in one end and out the other. The last box is a dead end — everything taken ends up thrown away.

The loop

Design the waste out from the start, and what comes back — repaired, reused, returned — feeds what comes next. Nothing has to be thrown away.

The Loop at Work: A Greenhouse

Johnny spent eight years at BrightFarms growing leafy greens — lettuce and spinach — hydroponically, in deep water culture and mobile gutter greenhouses. Seed, water, nutrients, and light go in; the crop comes out; and a working greenhouse is already a loop, because so much of what is left over goes straight back in:

Water & Nutrients

In deep water culture the nutrient water flows past the roots and around again, instead of draining away — and the North Carolina greenhouse also ran greywater filtration.

Recirculated
Plant Matter

Roots, trimmings, and spent plants are organic matter, not trash: returned as compost, they feed soil instead of landfill.

Returned
Knowledge

Environmental monitoring watches the climate, and what each crop cycle teaches — about light, temperature, and nutrition — tunes the next one.

Core Frameworks & Principles

Simultaneous Attention Framework

Finding solutions to improve people's lives through simultaneous attention to the three E's of sustainability:

Environment

Actively preserving and enhancing ecological systems.

Ecological Vitality
Economy

Fostering efficient, short-term and long-term market-based prosperity.

Market Resilience
Equity

Embedding equity so gains from environmental and economic progress are more fairly distributed.

Structural Fairness
Curitiba, Brazil

Example: Curitiba, Brazil, planned all three at once from the 1970s: the world's first bus rapid transit network, parks that double as flood control, and a Green Exchange that trades bags of trash for food and bus tokens — at its peak the city recycled about 70% of its waste.

Low Footprint
Gabe Brown's Ranch, North Dakota

Example — Cover Cropping: Gabe Brown's ranch near Bismarck, North Dakota: two decades of diverse cover crops, no-till, and livestock integration raised soil organic matter from under 2% to roughly 5–8%, and rain that once ran off now soaks in — with no synthetic fertilizer used since 2008.

The Honeynut Squash

Example — Plant Breeding: The Honeynut squash: Cornell breeder Michael Mazourek and chef Dan Barber selected for flavor first, creating a squash half a butternut's size with two to three times the beta-carotene — then co-founded Row 7 Seed Company to bring flavor-bred seed to market nationally.

KickStart MoneyMaker Pumps, Kenya

Example — Appropriate Tech: KickStart's MoneyMaker pumps in Kenya: human-powered irrigation pumps designed locally and sold — not given away — so farmers can grow in the dry season. More than 285,000 have been sold, and farmers who irrigate with them report raising their income from irrigation by nearly 500% on average.

Nature as Model, Measure, and Mentor

From biologist Janine Benyus's 1997 book Biomimicry: Innovation Inspired by Nature, advanced by the non-profit Biomimicry Institute: learn from 3.8 billion years of evolution — ask how nature solves it, then emulate the strategy. The AskNature database catalogues those strategies. biomimicry.org ↗

Nature as Model

Study nature's forms, processes, and systems, then imitate them to solve human problems.

Nature's Designs
Nature as Measure

Judge a design against nature's standard: what works, what is appropriate, what lasts.

Ecological Standard
Nature as Mentor

Value nature for what can be learned from it, not only for what can be extracted.

Learn, Don't Take
500 Series Shinkansen, Japan

Example: Japan's 500 Series Shinkansen: engineer Eiji Nakatsu modeled the train's long nose on a kingfisher's beak, which slips into water without a splash. The redesign ended the sonic boom at tunnel exits — and the train used about 15% less electricity while running 10% faster.

A Regenerative Economic System

From the Ellen MacArthur Foundation (founded 2010): an economic system that eliminates waste and pollution, circulates products and materials at their highest value, and regenerates nature — mapped in the butterfly diagram of technical and biological cycles. ellenmacarthurfoundation.org ↗

Eliminate Waste & Pollution

Design waste and pollution out from the start, instead of cleaning up after.

Design Out Waste
Circulate Products & Materials

Keep products and materials in use at their highest value, for as long as possible.

Highest Value, Longest Use
Regenerate Nature

Shift from extraction to practices that rebuild soils, ecosystems, and biodiversity.

Nature Positive
Schiphol Airport, Amsterdam

Example: Amsterdam's Schiphol Airport buys light instead of light bulbs: Philips keeps ownership of the fixtures, designs them to last longer and be taken back at end of life, and the airport cut its lighting electricity use by 50%.

Empire State Building, New York

Example: The Empire State Building: its deep energy retrofit earned LEED Gold in 2011 — then the tallest building in the U.S. to certify — cutting energy use by more than 38% and saving about $4.4 million a year.

Ultra-Low Energy Building Standard

A rigorous, voluntary international standard for energy efficiency in a building, drastically reducing its ecological footprint by maintaining an ultra-low heating and cooling demand.

Thermal Envelope

The First Passive House, Darmstadt

Example: The first Passive House — four row houses built in Darmstadt, Germany, in 1991 — has been measured ever since: it uses over 90% less heating energy than a conventional new building of its time, and re-testing 25 years later found it still performing as designed.

Circular Product Certification

From William McDonough and Michael Braungart's 2002 book Cradle to Cradle: Remaking the Way We Make Things, administered by the non-profit Cradle to Cradle Products Innovation Institute: it certifies products and materials — not buildings — at Bronze, Silver, Gold, or Platinum. Certified products can contribute points toward LEED certification. c2ccertified.org ↗

Example: Herman Miller's Mirra chair (2003), the first office furniture designed from the start under the Cradle to Cradle protocol: no PVC, 42% recycled content, 96% recyclable — and certified Gold or Silver depending on configuration.
