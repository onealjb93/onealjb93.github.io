My Health Committee

  1. Sleep 7–8 hours on a consistent schedule — keep a consistent wake time, and the phone out of the bedroom.
  2. Get daylight in the morning — outdoor light in the first hour after waking.
  3. Move daily, with regular cardio — take a 5–10 minute walk after meals, and stand or walk for 1–5 minutes every 30 minutes of sitting.
  4. Lift 2–3 times a week to maintain or build muscle.
  5. Train mostly easy, with some truly hard — periodized in cycles: base, build, peak, recover.
  6. Eat mostly whole, minimally processed foods — lots of plants, an extra serving of vegetables or beans a day, and oily fish a couple of times a week.
  7. Eat enough protein, spread across the day — and fuel for the work you do; avoid chronic under-fueling.
  8. Cut sugary drinks and ultra-processed snacks — swap sugary drinks for water, coffee, or tea.
  9. Keep alcohol low — under a weekly cap, with planned alcohol-free days.
  10. Don't smoke.
  11. Know your numbers — blood pressure, blood sugar, and cholesterol (ApoB if possible), and vitamin D; get bloodwork with a doctor (iron/ferritin, thyroid), not guesswork, and supplement vitamin D only if your level is low.
  12. Stay socially connected — reach out to a friend this week; schedule it.
  13. Creatine monohydrate, about 3–5 g a day, for muscle and strength — and be wary of expensive supplements and anyone selling certainty; test any new supplement in training first.
  14. Lower the load at home: cut blue light in the evening (dim the lights; blue-light blocking glasses 2–3 hours before bed), filter your drinking water (reverse osmosis, or a filter certified to remove PFAS), cook with cast iron, stainless steel, or ceramic — not non-stick — choose fragrance-free personal care and cleaning products, and don't heat food in plastic — use glass or steel.

Where They DisagreeAthletic Health

Even the athletic experts don't agree on everything:

  1. Zone 2 vs. more intensity — most agree you need both; the argument is the exact balance and definition.
  2. Protein — researchers cluster around 1.6–2.2 g/kg/day (0.73–1.0 g/lb) for athletes; some influencers recommend more. Higher isn't clearly better.
  3. Fasted or low-carb training — interesting for specific goals; not shown to beat well-fueled training for performance.
  4. Cycle-based training for women — promising for personalization, but the evidence is early.

Advanced Athlete ChecklistAthletic

From the 10 Athletic Health experts.

The Athletic Health experts, in training order:

  1. Periodize: base, build, peak, recover; rotate focus.
  2. Mostly easy, some truly hard (Seiler).
  3. Fuel for the work (Burke/Jeukendrup); avoid chronic under-fueling.
  4. Lift to maintain or build muscle (Schoenfeld, Phillips).
  5. Prioritize sleep and recovery (Mah).
  6. Plan heat and altitude (Périard).
  7. Get a sports-cardiology check if 40+, high volume, or any symptoms (Levine, Baggish).
  8. Track: HRV or resting heart rate, sleep, mood, performance.
  9. Get bloodwork with a doctor (iron/ferritin, vitamin D, thyroid, ApoB), not guesswork.
  10. Test any new supplement or fueling plan in training first.

Elite-athlete methods don't always transfer to non-elites. Many experts sell products — check conflicts of interest. And don't copy pro training volume without the recovery and support they have.
