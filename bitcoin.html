The card's dots and mini scales color each model by the segment the model's scaled value occupies on Johnny's ₿ Indicator's 0-to-100 scale. Each model's own chart colors the model by the model's own bands.

Power law — green in the bottom 5% of the range, yellow from 5% to 50% of the range, red above 50% of the range.

Production cost — green in the bottom 5% of the range, yellow from 5% to 50% of the range, red above 50% of the range.

Mayer — green at or under the 200-day average, yellow between the average and 2.4× the average, red at or over 2.4× the average.

MVRV — green when the market value of all bitcoin is no more than 15% above what holders paid for their bitcoin, yellow above 15% up to a Z-score of 7, red at a Z-score of 7 or higher.

Johnny's ₿ Indicator — each model is scaled from 0 to 100 against fixed anchors: the power law by the price's position in the power law's corridor; production cost by the price-to-cost ratio on a log scale (a ratio of 1 gives 0, a ratio of 10 gives 100); the Mayer Multiple on a line from 0.5 to 2.4 (0.5 gives 0, 2.4 gives 100); the MVRV Z-Score on a line from 0 to 7 (0 gives 0, 7 gives 100). The indicator's reading is the average of the four scalings.

A reading of 7.79 or lower is the buy band — the rare buy; a reading of 92.91 or higher is the sell band — the rare sell.

In the 564 weeks in the record — weeks, not days — the buy band fired in 13 weeks and the sell band fired in 6 weeks. The buy band fired on the day of the December 2018 bottom and on the day of the March 2020 bottom; the sell band fired on the day of the December 2013 top and on the day of the December 2017 top. No band fired in January 2015 or at either 2021 top.

A reading of the models, not a promise.

Method: production cost = the network’s daily electricity cost ÷ the coins mined that day. The electricity cost comes from difficulty — difficulty × 232 hashes per block × ~144 blocks a day — at $0.05 per kWh, with the mining fleet’s efficiency stepping down by era as hardware improved: about 1,000,000 J/TH in the CPU and GPU years, 10,000 with the first ASICs in 2013, 1,000 in 2014, 300 in 2016, 130 through the Antminer S9 years, 60 from 2019, 38 from 2021, 28 from 2023 and about 24 J/TH for today’s fleet. Coins mined = the block subsidy × ~144 blocks (50 BTC at the start, halving roughly every four years — 3.125 today) plus the day’s transaction fees. This is a model built on those stated assumptions, not a measurement — real miners pay different power prices and run different machines. Difficulty and fees: blockchain.info, Aug 2010 – Nov 10, 2026; spot, daily closes and the current difficulty then update live on load (Coinbase, mempool.space). The MVRV Z-Score is (market cap − realized cap) ÷ the standard deviation of market cap, the standard deviation built up day by day over the full history. Market cap and the MVRV ratio are CoinMetrics’ daily figures, from Jul 18, 2010. Realized cap itself is a paid metric on their API, so here it is worked out from the two figures they publish free — realized cap = market cap ÷ the MVRV ratio, the ratio’s own definition, so nothing is estimated. The line starts Feb 2, 2011, once the history behind the standard deviation is 200 days deep — a standard deviation over a days-old market would say nothing. If the live fetch fails, the chart and figures stand as of Nov 16, 2026. The 0 and 7 lines are the MVRV Z-Score’s long-standing reference points — not buy or sell signals.

The money in use today is designed to lose value. The dollar's plan is inflation of 2 percent a year — inflation means prices rising, so each dollar buys less — and measured inflation usually runs higher than 2 percent. The loss compounds.

  • A bank creates new money when the bank lends, and the bank charges interest on the money the bank created. The loan is a good thing when the loan starts a business and makes society productive — but the risk the interest pays for falls on the bank's capital, not on a saver's savings, because no saved money was lent.
▶The record, measuredRome, Byzantium, China, England, the hyperinflations, and the modern currencies — every figure with its source.

Money is a tool for moving value and holding it until you need it. It lets you trade without barter, measure different things in the same units, and save the value of work done today to spend later. A good money does those three jobs well.

Today's money has a flaw built in: it can be made at will. When more money is created faster than the world makes things, each unit buys less. The target is 2% inflation a year, but it often runs higher, and people see it in their own lives — food, energy, and housing costing more each year. Money saved for later quietly buys less than the money that was put away. So saving alone no longer keeps you even — to hold on to what you earned, you are pushed to invest it and take risks you never chose.

Rome — the silver coinage, Augustus to Gallienus

Parts of silver in every 100 parts of Roman coin metal

The graph plots the silver content of Rome’s standard silver coin — parts of silver in every 100 parts of coin metal. The number under each date is the pure silver in one coin, in grams; the AD 215 coin held about 2.6 grams of silver and was valued at 2 denarii, so the number under AD 215 is the silver for one denarius of face value, about 1.3 grams. Where the record gives a range for the silver content, the point is plotted at the middle of the range. Every point on the graph is one row of the table in the full record below the graph.

Across roughly 250 years, the silver in Rome's standard silver coin fell from about 98 parts in 100 to about 2.5 parts in 100 — a loss of roughly 97% of the coin's fine silver per unit of face value. The first cut was Nero's, in AD 64; the collapse to a silver-washed bronze coin came in the AD 260s.

Byzantium — the solidus, the coin that was not cut

Gold content of the solidus, in carats out of 24

The solidus held about 24 carats from AD 312 to the 1030s — roughly 700 years — then fell to 8 carats by 1081. Every point on the graph is one row of the table in the full record below the graph, except the table’s last row: Alexios I (1081–1092) is not plotted, because the table gives that row as a range, 0 to 8 carats, and the solidus was abolished in 1092.

The solidus held its weight and its near-pure gold fineness for roughly 700 years — the longest-lived sound coin in this record. Debasement began only in the 1030s–1040s, and once debasement began, the coin lost about two-thirds of its gold fineness in about 50 years. The money that was not debased did not lose value; the losses start in the same decade the cutting starts.

China — paper money's first full cycle

DateMeasured fact
1023The Song government takes over paper money and issues the state jiaozi — the world's first government paper money — initially in limited, term-limited issues
1260The Yuan government of Kublai Khan issues the Zhongtong notes; the notes lose value, and a second issue follows in 1287 — the Zhiyuan notes, exchanged at 1 new note for 5 old notes
1375The Ming government issues the Da Ming baochao — paper notes the government did not exchange for metal, with no limit on how many could be printed; the 1-guan note is set at 1,000 copper cash
By 1394The baochao trades at less than one fifth of face value
By 1425The baochao trades at about 2% of face value
1430s–1440sThe baochao is effectively abandoned; China runs on silver and copper cash, and the Chinese state does not return to paper money for centuries

The first civilization to run government paper money ran the full cycle: issue, over-issue, a fall to about 2 percent of face value within 50 years of the Ming issue of 1375, abandonment, and a return to metal money.

England — the Great Debasement, 1544–1551

Parts of silver in every 100 parts of English coin metal

English coin held the sterling standard — 92.5 parts of silver in every 100 parts of coin metal — from the 12th century until 1544. From 1544 to 1551 the silver in English coin fell to 25 parts in every 100 parts of coin metal. Every point on the graph is one row of the table in the full record below the graph.

In seven years the English Crown cut the silver in England's coins from 92.5 percent to 25 percent — the coins kept the same face value and the same royal portrait while about three quarters of the silver was removed.

Productivity vs. the dollar's buying power — since 1947

Productivity — what an hour of work producesBuying power — what $1 buys

People get better at making things every year, so things cost less to produce and life is better than ever. The money’s design taxes that progress: prices rise every year even though production gets cheaper, so the cheapening arrives as more expensive goods.

▶The record in the United StatesThe money supply and the federal debt, the prices of a weekly basket, and the big costs of a life — every figure with its source.

Since 1947, an hour of work produces about 5.3× more — the force pushing prices down. Over the same years, the dollar's buying power fell about 93% — a 1947 dollar buys about 7¢ today. The distance between those two lines is the printing. That gap is your purchasing power. It doesn't drain in a straight line — it compounds, and at the average pace since 1947 it halves about every 20 years. The halving pace has not been steady across the years. From the 2020 annual average to the 2024 annual average, the Consumer Price Index — the Bureau of Labor Statistics' measure of consumer prices — rose 4.9% a year on average; a 4.9% yearly rise, repeated every year, halves the dollar's buying power in about 14 years, faster than the average pace since 1947 of about 20 years. The fastest year of the 2020s was 2022, when consumer prices rose 8.0% from the 2021 annual average to the 2022 annual average; the 2021 and 2022 price rises have not reversed, and the Consumer Price Index at the 2024 annual average stood 22.7% above the Consumer Price Index at the 2019 annual average. The most recent full year ran slower: consumer prices rose 2.9% from the 2023 annual average to the 2024 annual average, and a 2.9% yearly rise, repeated every year, halves the dollar's buying power in about 24 years.

The Federal Reserve's H.6 release puts M2 — the money in checking accounts, savings accounts, and money market funds — at $4.67 trillion in January 2000 and $23.41 trillion in September 2026, and the M2 supply rose 40.5% in the 25 months from February 2020 ($15.49 trillion) to March 2022 ($21.77 trillion). The Treasury Department's Debt to the Penny record puts total federal debt at $5.66 trillion on December 29, 2000, $27.75 trillion on December 31, 2020, and $40.66 trillion on November 16, 2026, and the Treasury Department's Monthly Treasury Statement puts gross interest on Treasury debt securities — the total interest paid on Treasury debt securities before anything is subtracted — at $1.216 trillion for the fiscal year ended September 30, 2025. The Bureau of Labor Statistics' food-at-home index — the Consumer Price Index for groceries — rose 11.4% from the 2021 annual average to the 2022 annual average, and the food-at-home index stood 22.5% higher at the 2024 annual average than at the 2020 annual average, against a 21.2% rise in consumer prices overall across the same four years.

The new house itself grew — the Census Bureau measures the median new single-family house at 1,525 square feet in 1973 and 2,467 square feet in 2015 — and the growth in the house is far smaller than the growth in the price of the house. Medical care in 2024 is not medical care in 1970; the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services' figures measure spending, and the spending rose from 6.9% of gross domestic product (the total value of everything the United States produces in a year) in 1970 to 18.0% in 2024. The college figures are the tuition and required fees colleges charge, before grant aid: the National Center for Education Statistics records that at private nonprofit four-year colleges in 2022-23, 84.1% of first-time, full-time students received a grant from the college, averaging $26,000 among the students who received a grant, so the average student's bill is lower than the charge — and the charge is the price every family reads first.

▶The switch to a currency without the critical flaw(s) of all money known to man throughout history

Bitcoin was invented to be a currency without that flaw — the flaw that has existed in money throughout history. Only 21 million will ever exist, and no one can change that number. Money that cannot be printed lets the cheapening show as falling prices.
