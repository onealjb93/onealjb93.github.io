How to understand Bitcoin's current value

Four of the best-known Bitcoin indicators — the power law, production cost, the Mayer Multiple, the MVRV Z-Score. Choose one below: each sets today's price against Bitcoin's own history a different way. Low in its range has historically been the cheap zone; high, the stretched one. History, not a prediction — the decision stays yours.

The Power Law Chart

Bitcoin's price has spent fifteen years oscillating around a single straight line. Plot price against time since the genesis block with both axes logarithmic, and the chaos of four boom-and-bust cycles averages out into a power law — price ≈ 10−16.493 × days5.688. Physicist Giovanni Santostasi formalised the model in 2018; the fit still explains about 96% of the variance in log price (R² ≈ 0.96).

Actual price (orange) against the model's fair-value trend (white), with the historical support floor (green) and ceiling (red). Both axes are logarithmic, so the power law is a straight line. Dashed lines past today are the model projected forward — not a prediction.

A refit on the same data gives an exponent of ≈ 5.6 with R² ≈ 0.96 — the same picture; the exact exponent drifts as new data arrives (about 5.8 in 2022, about 5.6–5.7 today). Live daily-refit chart: bitcoinpowerlaw.com.

The Production Cost Chart

Actual price (orange) against the estimated cost of producing one bitcoin (green) — the network’s electricity bill for a day, divided by the coins mined that day. Dashed lines sit 10%, 20% and 30% below the cost, and red dots mark the days the price closed under it. The price axis is logarithmic; time is linear.

Method: production cost = the network’s daily electricity cost ÷ the coins mined that day. The electricity cost comes from difficulty — difficulty × 232 hashes per block × ~144 blocks a day — at $0.05 per kWh, with the mining fleet’s efficiency stepping down by era as hardware improved: about 1,000,000 J/TH in the CPU and GPU years, 10,000 with the first ASICs in 2013, 1,000 in 2014, 300 in 2016, 130 through the Antminer S9 years, 60 from 2019, 38 from 2021, 28 from 2023 and about 24 J/TH for today’s fleet. Coins mined = the block subsidy × ~144 blocks (50 BTC at the start, halving roughly every four years — 3.125 today) plus the day’s transaction fees. This is a model built on those stated assumptions, not a measurement — real miners pay different power prices and run different machines. Difficulty and fees: blockchain.info, Aug 2010 – Nov 10, 2026; spot, daily closes and the current difficulty then update live on load (Coinbase, mempool.space). If the live fetches fail, the Nov 13, 2026 figures stand, date included.

The Mayer Multiple Chart

The day’s price divided by its 200-day average — Trace Mayer’s measure of how far the price has stretched from its own recent history. Under the dashed 1.0 line, the price sits below its average; over the dashed 2.4 line, it has run far ahead of it.

The MVRV Z-Score Chart

The market’s value for all bitcoin against the value holders actually paid — every coin priced at the last time it moved, summed over all coins. The Z-Score is the gap between the two, divided by the standard deviation of market value across its whole history. A gauge by Murad Mahmudov and David Puell. Over the dashed 7 line has historically been top territory; under the dashed 0 line, bottom territory.

The MVRV Z-Score is (market cap − realized cap) ÷ the standard deviation of market cap, the standard deviation built up day by day over the full history. Market cap and the MVRV ratio are CoinMetrics’ daily figures, from Jul 18, 2010. Realized cap itself is a paid metric on their API, so here it is worked out from the two figures they publish free — realized cap = market cap ÷ the MVRV ratio, the ratio’s own definition, so nothing is estimated. The line starts Feb 2, 2011, once the history behind the standard deviation is 200 days deep — a standard deviation over a days-old market would say nothing. If the live fetch fails, the chart and figures stand as of Nov 16, 2026. The 0 and 7 lines are the gauge’s long-standing reference points — not buy or sell signals.

How to read it

  • Below the trend — the model frames Bitcoin as undervalued relative to its fifteen-year path. Deep below trend, near the green floor, has historically been the accumulation zone.
  • Above the trend — the model frames it as stretched. Bull-market peaks have topped out lower each cycle relative to trend (about 11× in 2013, 6.6× in 2017, 3.6× in 2021, about 1.4× in 2024–25).
  • Where it stands now — spot sits below the trend line but just above the support floor. The model says how stretched price is, never when it will move.

Honest limitations

  • A fit to the past, not a law of nature. Fifteen years and roughly four cycles is a striking sample, not a guarantee. Regulation, a technical failure, or a better competitor could bend the curve.
  • The exponent is not a constant. It is a regression estimate that shifts as data arrives, and different starting dates give anything from about 4.9 to 5.9. Distant projections swing wildly with small changes.
  • Wide bands. "Within −60% to +150% of trend" is a huge range in the short term. This is a compass for years, not a trading signal for Tuesdays.
  • Not financial advice — one lens among many.
