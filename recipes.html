Evidence-Informed Cooking & Nutrition Tips

From the Evidence-Informed Nutrition and Cooking Guide — themes drawn from the work of Rhonda Patrick, Peter Attia, Andrew Huberman, and Jo Robinson (Eating on the Wild Side), plus mainstream dietary research. Not medical advice.

How to read the evidence tags: [Strong] Supported by large trials or consistent long-term evidence. [Moderate] Reasonable human evidence, but smaller or less consistent. [Limited] Mostly lab, mechanistic, or small-study evidence. Low risk, uncertain payoff.

Two things to keep in mind: changes in nutrient absorption or biomarkers don’t automatically mean fewer diseases or a longer life — and overall dietary pattern matters far more than any single food or trick.

Top 10: Start Here (10 tips)
  1. Eat plenty of fiber, aiming for 30 g or more per day from vegetables, legumes, fruit, whole grains, nuts, and seeds. [Strong]
  2. Make extra virgin olive oil your main everyday fat. [Strong]
  3. Eat legumes (lentils, beans, chickpeas) several times a week. [Strong]
  4. Eat fatty fish (salmon, sardines, trout, herring) twice a week. [Strong]
  5. Get enough protein (roughly 1.2 to 1.6 g/kg body weight for most active adults), spread across 3 to 4 meals. [Moderate to Strong]
  6. Minimize ultra-processed foods, added sugar, and excess sodium. [Strong]
  7. Eat cruciferous vegetables regularly, and chop them ahead of cooking. [Moderate]
  8. Eat a variety of colorful fruits and vegetables, fresh or frozen. [Strong]
  9. Walk 10 to 15 minutes after meals when you can. [Moderate]
  10. Limit alcohol. Less is better. [Strong]
1. Get More From Plant Compounds (5 tips)
  1. Chop and rest. Chop or crush broccoli, garlic, and onions, then wait about 10 minutes for garlic and onions and 30 to 40 minutes for broccoli before cooking. This lets enzymes (myrosinase in cruciferous vegetables, alliinase in garlic) form protective compounds such as sulforaphane and allicin before heat deactivates them. [Limited to Moderate]
  2. Eat broccoli sprouts. They are very rich in glucoraphanin, the precursor to sulforaphane, which activates the Nrf2 antioxidant-response pathway. Add them raw to salads, eggs, sandwiches, and lentils. [Limited to Moderate]
  3. Add mustard powder to cooked crucifers. If broccoli has been cooked (which destroys myrosinase), about ½ to 1 teaspoon of mustard powder restores enzyme activity. It isn’t needed for raw broccoli. [Limited]
  4. Eat purple and deeply colored produce. Red cabbage, blueberries, blackberries, purple carrots, and purple potatoes contain anthocyanins, which are associated with vascular and metabolic health. [Moderate]
  5. Include onions regularly. They provide quercetin and sulfur compounds. Eat them raw in salads or gently cooked in soups, beans, and lentils. [Limited to Moderate]
2. Choose and Store Produce Well (7 tips)
  1. Favor darker, more colorful varieties. Red leaf lettuce, dark cherries, berries, and smaller tomatoes often contain more anthocyanins and other polyphenols than pale or large varieties. [Limited]
  2. Tearing lettuce may help. Some research suggests that damaging leaves ahead of time can raise certain antioxidants. The evidence is thin, so treat it as optional. [Limited]
  3. Eat delicate produce sooner. Spinach, asparagus, and broccoli lose nutrients during storage, so buy smaller amounts more often. [Moderate]
  4. Store tomatoes at room temperature until ripe. Refrigeration dulls flavor and texture. Refrigerate once ripe if you need more time.
  5. Store potatoes cool and dark, not refrigerated. Cold can convert starch to sugar, changing flavor and cooking behavior. Avoid sunlight, which causes greening.
  6. Buy frozen produce freely. Frozen vegetables and berries are picked and frozen at peak ripeness and can be nutritionally equal or better than “fresh” that has traveled for days. [Strong]
  7. Wash produce under running water, before cutting. Don’t use soap or detergents.
3. Cook to Improve Nutrient Availability (9 tips)
  1. Cook tomatoes and carrots. Heat softens cell walls and increases availability of lycopene and beta-carotene. [Moderate]
  2. Pair fat with fat-soluble nutrients. Olive oil, avocado, nuts, eggs, or butter improve absorption of carotenoids and vitamins A, D, E, and K from tomatoes, carrots, and leafy greens. Finish vegetables with a drizzle of extra virgin olive oil. [Moderate]
  3. Cook spinach lightly. It cooks down so you can eat more. Boiling and then discarding the water reduces oxalates (relevant for people prone to kidney stones). Cooking alone doesn’t solve this. [Moderate]
  4. Steam cruciferous vegetables briefly. Short steaming (a few minutes) preserves more beneficial compounds than prolonged boiling. [Moderate]
  5. Roast or steam beets. They contain betalains and dietary nitrates and are easier to eat cooked.
  6. Cook, cool, and reheat potatoes. Cooling creates some resistant starch, which may modestly improve glucose responses and feed gut bacteria. The effect is real but modest, and reheating retains part of it. [Limited to Moderate]
  7. Soak and thoroughly cook beans and legumes. This reduces antinutritional factors, improves digestibility, and lowers gas-producing oligosaccharides. Dried kidney beans must be boiled thoroughly, as undercooked ones are toxic. [Moderate]
  8. Use cooking liquids. Nutrients that leach into water stay in soups, sauces, and stews.
  9. Vary your cooking methods. Rotate steaming, roasting, sautéing, pressure cooking, and raw preparation. Raw isn’t always healthier. Some nutrients are better preserved with minimal heat, others become more available with cooking.
4. Build a Healthier Gut Microbiome (3 tips)
  1. Eat a variety of fibers. Different bacteria use different fibers, so rotate oats, legumes, berries, vegetables, seeds, and whole grains. [Strong]
  2. Eat lentils, beans, and other resistant-starch foods often. Legumes, green bananas, and cooled potatoes or rice supply fermentable carbohydrates that support short-chain fatty acid production. [Moderate to Strong]
  3. Include fermented foods. Plain kefir, plain yogurt, kimchi, and sauerkraut provide live microbes. Choose refrigerated, unpasteurized versions with live cultures. Early research links fermented food intake with greater microbiome diversity and lower inflammation markers. [Moderate]
5. Cardiovascular and Metabolic Health (7 tips)
  1. Eat fatty fish at least twice a week. Sardines, salmon, trout, and herring provide EPA and DHA omega-3s. [Strong]
  2. Eat a variety of nuts and seeds. Walnuts, almonds, pumpkin seeds, chia, and flax supply healthy fats, fiber, minerals, and polyphenols. [Strong]
  3. Use extra virgin olive oil as your main cooking fat. It is a core component of Mediterranean-style eating. For high-heat cooking, it is more stable than commonly believed, but avoid smoking it. [Strong]
  4. Use vinegar with carb-heavy meals. Acetic acid may modestly lower post-meal glucose. Put balsamic or red wine vinegar in dressings. Don’t drink concentrated vinegar. The evidence comes from small, short studies. [Limited]
  5. Eat vegetables and protein before starches. This sequence can reduce post-meal glucose spikes. Evidence is mostly from small trials, largely in people with diabetes or prediabetes. [Limited to Moderate]
  6. Walk 10 to 15 minutes after meals. Working muscles take up glucose, blunting post-meal spikes. [Moderate]
  7. Minimize ultra-processed foods. Build meals around whole or minimally processed foods with recognizable ingredients, fiber, protein, and little added sugar. [Strong]
6. Protein, Muscle, and Recovery (6 tips)
  1. Spread protein across the day. Aim for roughly 25 to 40 g per meal across 3 to 4 meals rather than loading dinner. [Moderate]
  2. Include high-quality protein. Eggs, fish, poultry, dairy, beef, and legumes provide essential amino acids. Animal proteins are especially efficient for building and maintaining muscle. [Strong]
  3. Use Greek yogurt and cottage cheese. They are convenient, high-protein, calcium-rich options that reduce reliance on protein powders.
  4. Try wild game in moderation. Elk, venison, and bison are lean and rich in protein, iron, zinc, and B12. If the animal was harvested with lead ammunition, lead fragments can contaminate the meat. Ask about non-lead ammunition, trim generously around wound channels, and cook to safe internal temperatures.
  5. Rotate seafood. Sardines give omega-3s and calcium, oysters give zinc, and salmon gives EPA and DHA. Favor lower-mercury choices (sardines, salmon, trout, herring, mussels, oysters) and limit high-mercury fish such as swordfish, king mackerel, and bigeye tuna. Pregnant people and young children should follow official fish-consumption guidance.
  6. Consider creatine monohydrate. It has strong evidence for improving strength and resistance-training performance. A common dose is 3 to 5 g per day. People with kidney disease should check with a doctor first. [Strong]
7. Meal Timing and Everyday Habits (4 tips)
  1. Keep most eating within daylight hours. Circadian rhythms affect glucose regulation. Avoid making late-night eating a habit. [Moderate]
  2. Finish dinner 2 to 3 hours before bed when practical. This may support sleep quality and glucose regulation. [Limited to Moderate]
  3. Drink green tea between meals. It contains catechins such as EGCG. Drinking it away from meals lessens interference with non-heme iron absorption. It contains caffeine, and evidence for strong metabolic effects is modest. [Limited]
  4. Stay hydrated. Increase fluids when sweating heavily.
8. What to Limit (5 tips)

Most of this guide is about eating more of good things. These limits often matter more.

  1. Added sugar and sugary drinks. [Strong]
  2. Excess sodium, most of which comes from packaged and restaurant food. Cooking at home gives you control. [Strong]
  3. Alcohol. Risk rises with intake, and no amount is clearly beneficial. [Strong]
  4. Processed and cured meats such as bacon, sausage, and deli meats. [Strong]
  5. Repeatedly reheated cooking oils at high temperatures, which accelerates oxidation and degradation.
9. Nutrient-Absorption Tricks (5 tips)
  1. Pair vitamin C with plant-based iron. Lemon, kiwi, oranges, and bell peppers improve absorption of non-heme iron from lentils, beans, and vegetables. [Moderate to Strong]
  2. Combine turmeric with black pepper. Piperine can substantially raise curcumin levels in the body. This is an absorption effect, and increased absorption doesn’t automatically mean a health benefit. [Limited]
  3. Grind flaxseed. Whole seeds can pass through undigested. Ground flax provides more accessible ALA omega-3, fiber, and lignans. [Moderate]
  4. Get vitamin C with collagen. Vitamin C is required for normal collagen synthesis. A normal diet usually provides enough, so mega-doses aren’t needed. [Limited]
  5. Get magnesium from food. Pumpkin seeds, almonds, spinach, legumes, and whole grains are excellent sources. [Moderate]
10. Food Safety and Storage (4 tips)
  1. Cool and refrigerate cooked rice promptly. Cooked rice left at room temperature can grow Bacillus cereus, which produces toxins that reheating doesn’t destroy. Refrigerate within about an hour and reheat until steaming hot. Don’t leave rice out to “develop resistant starch.” This is the most important safety tip in this guide.
  2. Refrigerate nuts and ground seeds. Their unsaturated fats oxidize, especially after grinding.
  3. Store olive oil in a dark, tightly sealed bottle, away from heat, light, and air.
  4. Handle cooked and cooled starches safely. The same cooling-and-refrigeration rule applies to cooked potatoes and legumes.
11. Cautions and Special Situations (6 notes)
  • Warfarin or other blood thinners: Keep vitamin K intake (leafy greens, broccoli) consistent rather than suddenly changing it, and talk to your prescriber.
  • Thyroid conditions: Very large amounts of raw cruciferous vegetables, sprouts, or concentrated supplements may affect thyroid function in susceptible people. Normal food portions are generally fine.
  • Kidney disease or kidney stones: Discuss protein, creatine, oxalate-rich foods (spinach, beets, nuts), and potassium with a clinician.
  • Pregnancy: Follow guidance on fish mercury, unpasteurized foods, and food safety.
  • Iron overload or low iron: Iron-absorption strategies matter differently depending on your situation.
  • Supplements: Whole foods first. Check interactions with medications.

Why It's Healthy:

  • Red miso — fermented, umami-rich amino acids
  • Extra-virgin olive oil — stable monounsaturated fats that support heart health
  • Brussels sprouts — high in sulforaphane, which activates the Nrf2 antioxidant-response pathway
  • Roasting, not boiling — keeps more vitamin C, which helps absorb plant iron and supports collagen
  • Olive oil + sprouts — fat helps absorb carotenoids (~55% higher vs coconut oil, 2023 trial)
  • Pasture-raised chicken — more omega-3, which supports heart and brain health, than conventional
  • Organic — fewer pesticide residues; nutrition difference modest

💡 Suggestion: Halve the sprouts 30–40 minutes ahead of cooking — chopping crucifers early lets the protective compounds form before heat stops them. Chop and rest.

💡 Cooking tip: For the cooked sprouts, ½–1 tsp of mustard powder restores the enzyme that cooking destroys — an easy add if you like. Mustard powder.

Ingredients
  • ⅓ cup red miso & ⅓ cup maple syrup
  • ⅓ cup extra-virgin olive-oil & 1 tsp ground cumin
  • 12 oz Brussels sprouts (trimmed and halved)
  • 3 to 4 chicken breasts (~2 lbs, cubed into 1 ½-inch pieces)
Preparation
  1. Step 1: Heat oven to 425°F. Line a baking sheet with aluminum foil.
  2. Step 2: Whisk red miso, maple syrup, olive oil, and cumin in a bowl. Reserve a scant ¼ cup (3 tbsp) of mixture for later. Toss chicken and sprouts in the remaining marinade.
  3. Step 3: Spread everything in a single layer on the baking sheet, scraping remaining marinade on top.
  4. Step 4: Bake 8 mins. Remove, daub reserved 3 tbsp marinade on top, and bake 4 mins more.
  5. Step 5: Switch to broil for 1–2 mins until chicken is cooked through and slightly charred. Serve warm.
Substitutions: Chicken can be swapped for steak or salmon. Brussels sprouts can be swapped for green beans or mushrooms.

Why It's Healthy:

  • Cabbage — glucosinolates + vitamin C, kept raw; glucosinolates form sulforaphane, which activates the Nrf2 antioxidant-response pathway, and vitamin C helps absorb plant iron

Ingredients
  • 1 lb Taiwanese flathead cabbage or Napa cabbage
  • Kosher salt (Diamond Crystal) and black pepper
  • 2 medium garlic cloves (finely grated into paste)
  • 2 tbsp toasted sesame oil & 1 tbsp untoasted sesame seeds
Preparation
  1. Step 1: Trim outer leaves and remove the solid core from the cabbage.
  2. Step 2: Cut leaves into rough 1 ¼-inch squares; slice thick core-adjacent pieces into thin ¼-inch strips.
  3. Step 3: Wash, drain, and place cabbage in a large bowl. Sprinkle with 3 tbsp salt, scrunch with hands until mixed and beginning to soften, then let sit for 10 minutes.
  4. Step 4: Whisk garlic paste, sesame oil, ⅛ tsp salt, and ¼ tsp pepper in a small bowl.
  5. Step 5: Rinse salt off cabbage thoroughly under running water, drain well, and set aside.
  6. Step 6: Toss cabbage with dressing and sprinkle with sesame seeds right before serving. Best eaten fresh!
Tip: Taiwanese flathead cabbage offers looser, softer leaves, but Napa cabbage is an ideal substitute.

Why It's Healthy:

  • Chicken thighs — lean protein, amino acids for muscle
  • Black pepper — piperine slows liver enzymes, raises absorption of plant compounds (~20× curcumin in a 1998 study — purified piperine, large dose; a pinch won't match it)
  • Sourdough (with crusty bread option) — fermentation cuts phytate, freeing up more iron and zinc; GI ~54 vs ~75 white bread; modest edge
  • Butter + cream — swap in grass-fed butter for 2–3× CLA and more omega-3 than grain-fed; omega-3s support heart and brain health

💡 Suggestion: The recipe lists plain unsalted butter — use grass-fed butter instead for higher omega-3s and CLA than conventional butter, same usage.

Ingredients
  • 1 tbsp whole black peppercorns & 2 tbsp extra-virgin olive oil
  • 8 boneless, skinless chicken thighs (~2 lbs) & Salt
  • 2 tbsp unsalted butter & 2 tbsp minced shallot
  • 1 cup low-sodium chicken broth & ½ cup heavy cream
  • 3 thyme sprigs, 1 tbsp lemon juice, 1 tbsp chopped parsley
Preparation
  1. Step 1: Crush peppercorns coarsely in a bag with a mallet or saucepan.
  2. Step 2: Heat oil in a 12-inch cast-iron skillet over medium. Season chicken with salt, sear in two batches until golden (~5 mins per batch), and set aside. Pour off remaining oil.
  3. Step 3: Melt butter in skillet, cook shallot for 1 minute, then add broth, cream, thyme, and cracked peppercorns, scraping up pan browned bits.
  4. Step 4: Return chicken to skillet, bring to a simmer, and cook until internal temp reaches 165°F (6–8 mins).
  5. Step 5: Transfer chicken to plates. Stir lemon juice and parsley into the sauce until thickened, spoon over chicken, and serve.
Serving Option: Serve with crusty bread or egg noodles if desired.

Ingredients
  • 2 lb boneless, skinless chicken breasts or thighs
  • 2 tbsp extra virgin olive oil
  • Juice of 1 lime
  • 3–4 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1 tbsp ground cumin
  • 2 tsp paprika
  • 1 tsp dried oregano
  • 1 tsp salt
  • ½ tsp black pepper
Cilantro-Lime Green Sauce
  • 1 cup fresh cilantro
  • 1 jalapeño, roughly chopped
  • 1 garlic clove
  • Juice of 1 lime
  • ½ cup plain Greek yogurt
  • 1 tbsp extra virgin olive oil
  • ½ tsp salt
Preparation
  1. Step 1: Marinate the chicken. Mix olive oil, lime juice, garlic, cumin, paprika, oregano, salt and pepper. Coat chicken thoroughly. Marinate 20–30 minutes or longer in the refrigerator.
  2. Step 2: Make the green sauce. Blend cilantro, jalapeño, garlic, lime juice, Greek yogurt, olive oil and salt until smooth. Refrigerate.
  3. Step 3: Roast the chicken. Preheat oven to 425°F. Place chicken on a lightly oiled baking sheet. Roast approximately 18–25 minutes, depending on thickness, until the thickest part reaches 165°F.
  4. Step 4: Rest and serve. Let chicken rest 5 minutes before slicing. Generously spoon green sauce over the chicken.
Recommended Sides: Ginger-turmeric rice cooked in chicken broth; fresh cucumber and tomato salad; grilled zucchini or asparagus. The secret: Don't skimp on the green sauce! The combination of cumin-garlic chicken and cool, tangy cilantro-lime sauce makes this dish special.

  • Garlic — minced into the warm cream sauce
  • Lemon + leeks — vitamin C helps absorb the leeks’ non-heme iron and supports collagen

💡 Suggestion: The recipe adds the minced garlic straight into the warm cream — rest it about 10 minutes first and allicin forms before the heat. Chop and rest.

💡 Cooking tip: The butter and cream here do real work — fat helps absorb the leeks’ carotenoids and fat-soluble vitamins. Pair fat with fat-soluble nutrients.

💡 Suggestion: The recipe lists plain heavy cream — use grass-fed cream instead for higher omega-3s and CLA than conventional cream, same usage.

Ingredients
  • 4 large leeks
  • 1 cup heavy whipping cream
  • 2 tbsp butter
  • 1 garlic clove, finely minced
  • 1 tsp Dijon mustard
  • ½ tsp salt, or to taste
  • ¼ tsp black pepper
  • ½ tsp fresh thyme (or ¼ tsp dried)
  • ¾ cup shredded Gruyère cheese
  • 2 tbsp grated Parmesan cheese
  • Small squeeze of fresh lemon juice
Preparation
  1. Step 1: Prepare the leeks. Cut off roots and dark green tops. Slice the white and light green portions crosswise into ½-inch rounds. Separate the layers and rinse thoroughly to remove dirt. Drain and arrange in a buttered glass baking dish.
  2. Step 2: Make the cream sauce. In a small saucepan, gently warm the heavy cream, butter, garlic, Dijon mustard, salt, pepper and thyme. Stir until combined. Do not boil.
  3. Step 3: Assemble. Pour the cream mixture evenly over the leeks. They should be mostly coated.
  4. Step 4: First bake. Cover the baking dish tightly with foil. Bake at 375°F for 25 minutes.
  5. Step 5: Add cheese. Remove the foil. Sprinkle Gruyère and Parmesan evenly over the top.
  6. Step 6: Finish baking. Bake uncovered for another 15–20 minutes, until the leeks are completely tender and the cheese is golden and bubbling. If needed, bake another 5–10 minutes.
  7. Step 7: Rest and serve. Let rest for 5–10 minutes. Finish with a small squeeze of fresh lemon juice and freshly cracked black pepper.
Serving Notes: Excellent alongside roasted chicken, steak, pork or fish. The leeks become sweet and tender, while the cream, garlic, Dijon and Gruyère create a rich French-style sauce. The secret: Cut the leeks into uniform ½-inch rounds, cover during the initial bake to soften them, then uncover with cheese to create that beautiful golden top.

Ingredients
  • 4–5 packed cups fresh baby arugula (about 4 oz)
  • ¼ cup shaved Parmigiano-Reggiano
  • 1–2 tbsp pine nuts, lightly toasted
  • Freshly cracked black pepper
  • Flaky sea salt
Fresh Lemon-Garlic Dressing
  • 2½ tbsp good-quality extra-virgin olive oil
  • 1 tbsp freshly squeezed lemon juice
  • ¼ small garlic clove, freshly pressed
  • ½ tsp finely grated fresh lemon zest
  • Freshly cracked black pepper
  • Small pinch of sea salt
Preparation
  1. Step 1: Toast the pine nuts. Place in a dry skillet over medium-low heat for 2–3 minutes, stirring frequently until lightly golden and fragrant. Cool.
  2. Step 2: Make the dressing. Whisk together olive oil, lemon juice, pressed garlic, lemon zest, salt, and pepper. Let sit for 3–5 minutes.
  3. Step 3: Prepare the greens. Make sure arugula is thoroughly washed and dried. Place in a large salad bowl.
  4. Step 4: Dress the salad. Drizzle dressing over the arugula a little at a time. Toss gently until lightly coated.
  5. Step 5: Finish. Add shaved Parmesan and toasted pine nuts. Finish with freshly cracked black pepper and flaky sea salt. Serve immediately.
Chef's Notes: Use a vegetable peeler for beautiful, wide Parmesan shavings. Start with less dressing; arugula should be lightly coated, never soggy. Use just a little garlic so it doesn't overpower the lemon and Parmesan. Good extra-virgin olive oil makes a noticeable difference. A few drops of aged balsamic are optional, but lemon should remain the dominant flavor.

Choose ONE Fruit
  • Peach: 1¼ lb ripe fresh peaches, peeled and pitted.
  • Strawberry: 1¼ lb ripe fresh strawberries, washed and hulled.
Fruit Preparation
  • ¼ cup granulated sugar
  • 1 tbsp fresh lemon juice
  • 1 tbsp mild honey
Creamy Custard Base
  • 2 cups whole milk
  • 1½ cups heavy cream
  • 6 large egg yolks
  • ¼ cup granulated sugar
  • ¼ tsp kosher salt
  • 1 tsp pure vanilla extract
Preparation
  1. Step 1: Prepare the Fruit. Peel and pit 1¼ lb peaches OR wash and hull 1¼ lb strawberries. Slice all the fruit. Combine in a saucepan with: ¼ cup granulated sugar; 1 tbsp fresh lemon juice; 1 tbsp mild honey. Let macerate for 20 minutes. Important: Do not reserve any fruit pieces. All the fruit will be puréed completely smooth. We want absolutely no chunks in the finished ice cream.
  2. Step 2: Cook and Concentrate the Fruit. Cook the fruit mixture over medium-low heat for approximately 15–20 minutes, stirring frequently. Cook until the fruit is very soft and the mixture is thick, syrupy and noticeably reduced. Allow excess water to evaporate. This is especially important with strawberries, which contain considerable water. Do not aggressively boil or caramelize. Let cool for 10 minutes. Transfer the entire mixture, including its syrup, to a blender. Blend until completely smooth. Pass through a fine-mesh strainer to remove strawberry seeds, peach fibers and any remaining solids. Refrigerate.
  3. Step 3: Make the Custard. Combine in a saucepan: 2 cups whole milk; 1½ cups heavy cream; ¼ cup granulated sugar; ¼ tsp kosher salt. Heat over medium heat until steaming. Do not boil. In a separate bowl, whisk 6 large egg yolks. Slowly whisk 1 cup of the hot dairy mixture into the egg yolks. Pour the tempered egg mixture back into the saucepan with the remaining 2½ cups dairy. Cook over medium-low heat, stirring constantly, until the custard reaches approximately 175°F and coats the back of a spoon. Do not boil.
  4. Step 4: Strain and Combine. Pour the custard through a fine-mesh strainer into a clean bowl. Add: All of the cooked, puréed and strained fruit mixture; 1 tsp pure vanilla extract. Whisk thoroughly until completely uniform. The finished base should be entirely smooth, with no fruit chunks, seeds or fibers.
  5. Step 5: Chill Thoroughly. Place the bowl over an ice bath and stir until completely cool. Cover and refrigerate for at least 6 hours, preferably overnight. The mixture must be thoroughly cold before churning. This helps the Waring freeze the mixture faster, producing smaller ice crystals.
  6. Step 6: Churn in the Waring 166P. Pour the thoroughly chilled mixture into the freezing canister. Do not exceed the maximum fill level. Install the dasher and lid. Prepare the ice-and-salt freezing bath according to the Waring 166P instructions. Churn approximately 20–30 minutes, or until thick and creamy, similar to firm soft serve. Do not rely exclusively on the clock. Watch the consistency.
  7. Step 7: Harden. Immediately transfer the finished ice cream to a shallow, airtight freezer container. Smooth the surface. Press parchment paper or plastic wrap directly against the ice cream. Seal and freeze for at least 4 hours, preferably overnight. Keep the container toward the back of the freezer, where the temperature is most stable. Let stand for approximately 5–10 minutes before scooping.

Ingredients
  • 3 to 4 pounds potatoes, peeled and cut into ¼-inch slices
  • Kosher salt and black pepper
  • 2 tablespoons unsalted butter
  • 2 to 3 cups half-and-half or heavy cream
  • ¼ cup grated Parmesan or Gruyère
  • Pinch of nutmeg
Preparation
  1. Step 1: Heat oven to 400 degrees. Seasoning each layer with salt and pepper as you go, layer the potatoes in a 10- or 12-inch ovenproof skillet.
  2. Step 2: Dot the top with 2 tablespoons butter. Pour in half-and-half. (It should come about ¾ of the way to the top.)
  3. Step 3: Bring to a boil over medium-high heat. Reduce to low and cook for 10 minutes, uncovered, until the half-and-half has reduced by about half.
  4. Step 4: Transfer to the oven and bake until the top is golden brown, about 10 minutes. Reduce heat to 300 degrees and cook until the potatoes are fork tender and the top is nicely browned, about 8 minutes more. Top with grated cheese and return to the oven until the cheese is melty and golden brown, about 3 to 5 minutes. Sprinkle with a pinch of nutmeg, if using, and serve.

Why It's Healthy:

For the Ribs
  • 1 tablespoon garlic powder
  • 1 tablespoon onion powder
  • 1 tablespoon smoked paprika
  • 1 teaspoon kosher salt (such as Diamond Crystal)
  • ½ teaspoon black pepper
  • 3 to 4 pounds St. Louis- or country-style pork ribs, cut into individual ribs (see Tips)
  • 2 tablespoons vegetable or canola oil for searing, plus more if necessary
  • 1 medium onion, sliced
  • 4 cloves garlic, smashed
  • 1 cup pure pineapple juice
  • ⅓ cup soy sauce
  • 2 tablespoons apple cider vinegar
  • 1 tablespoon Worcestershire sauce
For the Glaze
  • ½ cup ketchup
  • ¼ cup honey
  • 2 to 3 tablespoons (light or dark) brown sugar
  • 1 tablespoon unsalted butter
For serving
  • Cooked white rice, cornbread or coleslaw, optional.
Preparation
  1. Step 1: Prepare the ribs. Heat the oven to 300 degrees. In a small bowl, stir together the garlic powder, onion powder, smoked paprika, salt and pepper. Pat the ribs dry with paper towels and rub the seasoning evenly over every side.
  2. Step 2: Heat the oil in a large Dutch oven over medium-high until shimmering. Working in batches so the pot is not crowded and lowering the heat as necessary to avoid scorching, sear the ribs on all sides until deeply browned, 2 to 3 minutes per side. Transfer to a plate as they finish.
  3. Step 3: Reduce the heat to medium. Add the onion and garlic to the pot and cook, stirring occasionally, until softened and just beginning to color, 2 to 3 minutes. Pour in the pineapple juice, soy sauce, vinegar and Worcestershire sauce, scraping up any browned bits from the bottom of the pot.
  4. Step 4: Return the ribs and any accumulated juices to the pot, nestling them into the liquid — it should come about halfway up the ribs. Cover and transfer to the oven. Bake for 1 hour, remove the ribs from the oven and gently toss and rearrange. Return to oven and cook another 45 minutes to 1 hour, until very tender but not falling off the bone. Remove the Dutch oven from the oven and uncover it. Raise the oven temperature to 400 degrees.
  5. Step 5: While the oven heats, prepare the glaze. Combine the ketchup, honey, brown sugar and butter in a small saucepan. Bring to a simmer over medium heat, reduce heat to medium-low and cook, stirring occasionally, for about 5 minutes, until the glaze is thick enough to coat the back of a spoon.
  6. Step 6: Transfer the ribs to a rack set on a baking sheet. Pour or brush the glaze over the ribs, turning each piece to coat. Return the ribs to the oven and cook for 5 to 7 minutes, until caramelized. If you'd like, skim the fat from the braising liquid in the pot. Serve the ribs hot, with the braising liquid spooned over rice or reduced into a sauce on the side.
Tips: Country-style ribs or a St. Louis cut both work. Baby backs cook a touch faster — start checking at the 1 hour 30 minute mark. Ask the butcher to cut the rack into individual ribs, or do it yourself with a sharp chef's knife between the bones before seasoning.

How your targets are calculated

Calories: basal metabolic rate (BMR) by Mifflin-St Jeor — men: 10 × weight (kg) + 6.25 × height (cm) − 5 × age + 5; women: the same, minus 161 (Mifflin et al., American Journal of Clinical Nutrition, 1990) — multiplied by an activity factor: sedentary 1.2, lightly active 1.375, moderately active 1.55, very active 1.725, extra active 1.9. Without height, BMR is estimated from weight alone (24 kcal/kg/day for men, 22 for women) and labelled as a rough estimate. Protein: 1.2–1.6 g per kg of body weight per day (Dietary Guidelines for Americans 2025–2030), scaled by activity from 1.2 (sedentary) to 1.6 (very/extra active). Fat: 20–35% of calories and carbohydrate: 45–65% (acceptable ranges, Dietary Guidelines); the dots use the middle of each range. Fiber: about 14 g per 1,000 kcal. Iron, calcium, vitamin D, vitamin C, vitamin A and potassium use age- and sex-specific recommended intakes (NIH Office of Dietary Supplements); sodium (2,300 mg), saturated fat (under 10% of calories) and cholesterol (300 mg) are daily limits. Total sugars has no daily value. Targets are estimates for a generally healthy adult, not medical advice.
